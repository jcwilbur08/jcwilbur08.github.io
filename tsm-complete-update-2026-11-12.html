Since October 15

What's new: a record month, a loaded board meeting, a political rout

Three things happened that matter. October sales beat the run-rate the Q4 guide needs. The November 10 board meeting was the most substantive company news of the month — and it was bullish. And on November 5 the stock lost 10.78% in a day for reasons that had nothing to do with TSMC.

October revenue — verified, and ahead of the guide's pace

TSMC reported October 2026 consolidated revenue of NT$586.89 billion, up 59.7% year over year and 14.7% month over month against September's NT$511.86 billion — a new all-time monthly record, above the prior record of NT$514.81 billion set in August. January–October revenue reached NT$4,485.62 billion, up 43.3% year over year. The monthly release is unaudited and revenue-only, with no node, customer, or margin split.

The November 10 board meeting

Record dividend

NT$9.00 per share for Q3 2026, up 28.6% from NT$7.00 and 50% above the NT$6.00 paid for Q3 2025. Ex-date March 11, 2027; payment April 8, 2027. The payout is 30.6% of Q3 EPS.

~$40.93B appropriated

Capital appropriations for advanced-technology capacity, advanced packaging, fab construction, and 2027 R&D and sustaining capex. An appropriation, not a new annual guide — but a management team does not commit $41 billion against a demand cliff.

AI-ecosystem stakes

Up to $500 million each in Anthropic common shares at its IPO price and OpenAI preferred stock, plus up to $500 million for a semiconductor-ecosystem investment company. These are ceilings, not completed purchases; no rationale, board seats, or commercial commitments were disclosed.

The current quarterly dividend before the raise is NT$7.00 per ordinary share for Q2 2026, with an ex-dividend date of Thursday, December 10, 2026, record date December 16, and payment January 7, 2027; the ADS amount is approximately $1.0854, down from $1.1136 the prior quarter on FX, not on a NT$ cut. On a trailing basis the ADR dividend is $4.49 for a 1.11% yield at the close; annualising the new NT$9.00 quarterly run-rate gives roughly $5.48 per ADR, about 1.35% at $405.49 — desk arithmetic, since the actual ADS payment depends on the exchange rate at payment.

Capacity signals worth carrying forward

  • 2nm capacity in 2027 is planned at more than double 2026, and 2nm already reached 8% of wafer revenue in Q3, its first full ramp quarter.
  • TSMC will build another 3nm fab in Tainan, volume production in the first half of 2028 — a fab it would normally skip, because 3nm demand is judged strong for many more years. A14 starts risk production in 2027, volume in 2028.
  • In Arizona, Fab 2 (N3) construction is complete with tool move-in under way and volume production pulled slightly ahead of schedule to 3Q 2027; total Arizona investment stands at $265 billion. The quantified cost: overseas-fab gross-margin dilution of roughly 2% in 2026, widening to 3–4% later as more US fabs ramp.
  • Reporting that TSMC is drafting plans for stacked 3D DRAM at Nvidia's request is unconfirmed — the company's spokesperson restated that TSMC "focuses on its core dedicated IC foundry business," neither confirming nor denying the planning. Treat it as an option, not a programme.
The largest customer

The Huang resignation: a governance shock, not — yet — a demand shock

On November 12, roughly 42 minutes before the US open, Nvidia announced that Jensen Huang had resigned as president, CEO, and director, effective immediately. Nvidia is TSMC's largest customer. The read-through for TSMC is second-order — but it is now the single largest unquantifiable in the thesis.

Nov 8
The investigation opensNvidia's board announced an independent investigation, with outside counsel, into reports Huang was targeted by a foreign-intelligence effort. The board said it had no reason to believe Nvidia secrets or US national security had been compromised, and expressed confidence in Huang. Huang apologised to employees for infidelity. No charges have been reported against him; China has denied the espionage allegations.
Nov 11
A second report; the board prepares to decideReporting of a second affair, involving an Nvidia vice president who reported to Huang, was followed by reports that the board was preparing to decide his future. The board met on November 11.
Nov 12
Resignation, effective immediatelyCFO Colette Kress becomes interim CEO while keeping the CFO role. Harvey C. Jones, a board member and former Synopsys CEO, becomes Nvidia's first-ever board chair. Huang, 63, co-founded Nvidia in 1993 and served 33 years as CEO; he holds about 3.61% of the company and also leaves the board. No reason was given in the announcement and no severance was disclosed.
Nov 17
The first hard testNvidia reports fiscal Q3 after the close — the first print under Kress, and the first hard demand data point since the resignation. Data-centre revenue against roughly $108–109 billion of guidance, the Vera Rubin ramp, and any change to the 2027 order outlook are what matter for TSMC.

What the tape said on the day

Nvidia fell more than 12–14% in early trading, touched $148.80, and closed at $166.19, down 4.34%; it had lost roughly $300 billion of market value since the investigation was announced. TSMC's ADR gapped down 4.5% at the open — and then held. Its low of $393.91 was within $0.71 of its open, and it recovered to close at $405.49, down 1.90% on below-average volume. Meanwhile AMD rose 5.86% and Broadcom 5.54%. That is a rotation within TSMC's customer base, not away from TSMC: AMD and Broadcom accelerators are also TSMC wafers.

What is reported, and what is not

Reported: Nvidia's board said operations and customer commitments continue without interruption, and next-generation chips are already in production. One Taiwanese supplier official put the industry view plainly: "We will support any production capacity Nvidia needs, but the new CEO may not be as driven as Jensen was." Not found, as of the November 12 close: any TSMC statement on Huang, any Nvidia order cancellation or cut to TSMC, any TSMC guidance change, and any analyst downgrade of TSM tied to the resignation.

The genuine risk is pace, not factories. Huang personally architected Nvidia's supplier network and was the loudest voice in Washington against tighter China chip curbs. A successor — internal or external, with no timeline announced — may commit more slowly, sequence products differently, or lose share to AMD and custom silicon. For TSMC, two of those three outcomes are roughly neutral, because the share lands on other TSMC customers. The outcome that hurts is the one where Nvidia's total order growth slows. Nothing yet shows that; November 17 is when the evidence starts.

Demand and the AI cycle

Capex still points up. Politics and memory now point back.

The demand engine behind TSMC has not changed direction: hyperscaler capex forecasts for 2027 cluster near a trillion dollars. What changed in November is that two constraints — US permitting politics and memory prices — became real enough to price.

The political constraint is now real

The November 3 midterms gave Democrats both houses of Congress. The Texas governor-elect campaigned on a data-centre moratorium; the Georgia and Ohio governor-elects pledged pauses; senior Democrats called for a national moratorium. UBS estimates roughly 30% of 2028 US data-centre capacity additions are at risk of pause or delay, and one research count found more than 500 local US data-centre bans or pauses by October 2026. This is a demand-timing risk, not yet a budget cut — no hyperscaler has lowered a capex guide — but it is the first real political stress test of the build-out, and the November 5 rout was its price discovery.

Memory: a two-sided risk, not a pure tailwind

Contract prices are forecast to rise 10–15% for conventional DRAM and 15–20% for NAND in Q4, with DRAM supply tight into 2027; the major memory makers are earning roughly 80% operating margins. The squeeze is visible at TSMC's customers: Apple guided its December-quarter gross margin to 45–46% citing memory costs first, Qualcomm warned on memory costs after its November 4 close, and Intel expects industry PC shipments to fall about 12% in 2026 and 10% in 2027 amid shortages.

For TSMC the negative is straightforward — pressure on the smartphone, PC, and IoT demand that still makes up roughly a quarter of revenue, and a higher bill of materials on every AI server, which can slow system shipments even when logic demand is strong. The offsets are strategic: the logic base die for Nvidia's custom NVHBM is to be made on TSMC's 3nm, and memory scarcity raises the value of anything TSMC can do at the scarce layer of the stack. Critically, memory has not yet dented TSMC's own book — October was a record month and Q3 HPC revenue grew 22% sequentially.

Broadcom–Anthropic: the threat that runs through TSMC

The $60 billion custom-chip programme flagged in October has firmed up, and it still does not route around TSMC — Broadcom designs the accelerators; TSMC manufactures them. Anthropic is expected to become Broadcom's largest customer in 2027 at roughly $60 billion of revenue, and a $63 billion financing structure to buy Broadcom accelerators and networking and lease them to Anthropic was documented on October 26, with Broadcom providing contingent residual-value support capped at roughly $43 billion. Anthropic's own prospectus shows at least $518 billion of AI-infrastructure spending over a decade, about 80% non-cancellable — and names TSMC as the concentration risk for its chip supply. Broadcom rose 5.54% on November 12 while Nvidia fell. Custom silicon is growing through TSMC, not around it — and the board's approved $500 million Anthropic stake now sits on the same side of that trade.

Competition: improving at the margin, not yet a threat

Samsung

Confirmed 2nm orders from large cloud and AI HPC customers in Q3, with 2nm in mass production — but yields were around 60% in late September, below the roughly 70% threshold most major designers require. A report that Samsung will make HBM base dies for AMD and Nvidia at its Taylor fab is unconfirmed by all parties.

Intel

18A is in high-volume manufacturing and costs have fallen about 40% since Q1 — for Intel's own products. Intel Foundry lost $1.9 billion in Q3 on only $411 million of external revenue, guided 2027 as an investment year with likely negative free cash flow, and has disclosed no major external foundry win.

TSMC's foundry share stands at roughly 70–73%, and its quarterly foundry revenue runs about eleven times Samsung's foundry unit. No evidence was found of a leading-edge customer defecting from TSMC in November. On packaging, TSMC does not disclose CoWoS capacity; supply-chain reporting of a possible 10–20% CoWoS price increase and 3–6% wafer price increases from January 2027 is unconfirmed by TSMC and is carried here as reporting, not guidance. Management's own line stands: demand is still above supply, and AI accelerator revenue is targeted to compound at a mid-to-high-50s rate through 2029.

Balance sheet and the guide in context

At September 30, cash and marketable securities were NT$4,016.98 billion against long-term interest-bearing debt of NT$818.38 billion — in US dollar terms, roughly $126.04 billion of cash against $25.68 billion of total debt, for net cash of $100.36 billion, or $19.35 per share. The current ratio was 2.6× and annualised ROE 45.5%. Q3 capex of NT$443.21 billion was about 30% of revenue, down from roughly 39% in Q2 — lumpy by quarter, not a slowdown.

The Q4 guide's midpoint implies +14.1% sequential revenue growth with gross margin of 66.0% at the midpoint — no rebound promised, because the 2nm ramp and overseas-fab dilution are stated drags, partly offset by pricing and productivity. The thesis, both ways

What strengthened, what weakened — and the two cases stated plainly

Strengthened since October 15

  • Hard demand data. October revenue was a record and ahead of the Q4 guide pace; Q3 beat the top of guidance.
  • Management's own signals. A record dividend, raised 28.6%, and roughly $40.9 billion of capacity appropriations.
  • Diversification within TSMC. The November 12 rotation — AMD and Broadcom up, Nvidia down — and the documented $63 billion Broadcom–Anthropic financing show demand broadening beyond one customer while still flowing through TSMC's fabs.
  • Pricing and mix. 2nm already at 8% of wafer revenue; reported, unconfirmed, packaging and wafer price increases point the same way.
  • Valuation. De-rated from roughly 24× to roughly 19.5× forward earnings while FY2027 consensus EPS is forecast to grow about 31%.

Weakened since October 15

  • Political risk is priced, not theoretical. A 10.78% single-day fall on moratorium politics; roughly 30% of 2028 US capacity additions estimated at risk.
  • Key-person risk at the largest customer. Huang's resignation, days before Nvidia's print, with no permanent successor and no 2027 order visibility yet.
  • The memory bottleneck. Surging DRAM and NAND prices are squeezing customer margins and can slow system shipments.
  • Geopolitics. An unannounced isolation-rehearsal around Taiwan in early November — a new form of the standing tail risk.
  • Technical damage. Below the 50-day average and 16.8% off the high, in a sector index still far below its June peak.

Bull case

TSMC is the indispensable manufacturer of the AI build-out: it holds more than 70% of the foundry market, its 2nm node is ramping faster than any prior node, HPC is 68% of revenue and growing 22% a quarter, October revenue set a record ahead of a Q4 guide that already implies 14% sequential growth, hyperscaler capex is forecast near $1 trillion in 2027, and the board has just raised the dividend to a record and committed roughly $41 billion of capacity — while the stock trades at roughly 19.5× forward earnings, its own average multiple, with the Street's average target at $586.69 and Morningstar fair value at $534, both well above the $405.49 close. Every plausible winner in AI — Nvidia, AMD, Broadcom, Google, Amazon — pays TSMC.

Bear case

The November tape shows the AI trade can lose a tenth of its value in a day on politics alone, and the politics have not gone away: a Democratic Congress, moratoria pledged in Texas, Georgia, and Ohio, hundreds of local bans, and roughly 30% of 2028 US capacity at risk all point to slower build-out timing, while the memory shortage squeezes customers' margins and raises system costs. At Nvidia, the founder-CEO who personally drove the order book has resigned amid a national-security-tinged scandal, with no permanent successor. TSMC's own margins are rolling over on the 2nm ramp with overseas dilution widening to 3–4%, its 2026–2028 capex will be significantly higher than the prior three years, and the $545 target requires a re-rating to roughly 24.4× FY2027 earnings — the mid-2026 peak multiple — in a market currently de-rating the group. Add the unannounced exercise around Taiwan, and the tail risks are fatter than they were on October 15, even though the quarter was a record.

Risk register

Risks, ranked — with the mitigant the evidence actually supports

Risk 1 · Highest

Customer concentration and key-person risk at Nvidia

Nvidia is TSMC's largest customer, and Huang's resignation creates commitment and cadence uncertainty at exactly the wrong moment. Mitigant: AMD, Broadcom, and custom-ASIC growth is also TSMC volume, the November 12 tape showed share rotating inside the customer base rather than leaving it, and no order cut has been reported. The mitigant holds only until November 17's print says otherwise.

Risk 2

Geopolitics — Taiwan Strait and US politics together

Early November brought an unannounced exercise simulating the surrounding and isolation of Taiwan — more than 100 naval and coast-guard vessels, four task forces including a carrier strike group, and 49 air sorties around Taiwan on November 2 — departing from the announced pattern of the last four major exercises; activity had returned to routine by November 9, and no TSMC operational impact was reported. The nearer-term change is American: moratorium politics now threatens build-out timing. Mitigant: the Arizona ramp, with Fab 2 entering volume production in 3Q 2027, diversifies production — slowly. A Trump–Xi meeting is expected at next week's APEC summit in Shenzhen.

Risk 3

The capex and AI-ROI cycle

If hyperscaler returns disappoint or permitting slows the build-out, TSMC's significantly higher 2026–2028 capex becomes the overhang. The first signal would be a soft 2027 capex guide in January. Mitigant: October revenue and the $40.9 billion appropriation say the order book is still full, and management's October line was that demand remains above supply.

Risk 4

Competition

Samsung's 2nm orders and yields are improving — roughly 60% against a roughly 70% customer threshold — and Intel's 18A is in high-volume manufacturing. Mitigant: neither has yet won a leading-edge external customer at scale, and TSMC's yield and scale lead is intact, with 2nm capacity set to more than double in 2027.

Risk 5

Memory costs

The DRAM and NAND surge squeezes customers and can cap AI-server shipments. Ranked last because it cuts both ways for TSMC: HBM base dies on its 3nm and the reported 3D DRAM exploration make scarcity partly an opening. Mitigant: memory has not yet appeared in TSMC's own order book — October was a record.

The desk's bottom line

Hold the Strong Buy at 8/10 and the $545 target, hold the 45 shares, and let the evidence dates do the work. The reported numbers since October 15 earned the rating's defence; the fatter risk picture is why nothing here reaches for more. If November revenue misses the NT$538 billion pace line, or Nvidia's print shows a demand break, conviction — not the business — is what gets re-underwritten first.
