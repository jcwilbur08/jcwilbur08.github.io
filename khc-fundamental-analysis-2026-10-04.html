1. The quarter: two beats in a row, but profits are the tell

Q2 2026 — reported August 5, 2026

Kraft Heinz reported net sales of $6.262 billion, down 1.4% year over year, beating the $6.12 billion consensus by about 2.3%. Organic net sales fell 1.3%, a beat against models that had penciled in roughly -3.8%, built from pricing of +1.3 percentage points across all segments offset by volume and mix of -2.6 points. Adjusted EPS of $0.56 beat the $0.53 consensus by nearly 6% but fell 18.8% year over year. The composition of the beat matters: adjusted gross margin was flat at 34.1%, while adjusted operating income fell 18.4% to $1.041 billion and adjusted operating margin compressed 346 basis points to 16.6%. Sales are stabilizing; profits are funding the reset.

Regionally, North America ($4.626 billion) posted organic sales of -2.7%, with pricing of +1.1 points overwhelmed by volume and mix of -3.8 points — volumes remain the binding constraint. International developed markets were roughly flat organically at -0.7%, while emerging markets grew 8.5% organically on both price and volume. On a GAAP basis the company reported a $6.4 billion operating loss, driven by a $7.4 billion non-cash impairment charge — a reminder that trailing GAAP earnings are not a usable valuation anchor here.

Management updated full-year guidance in three directions: adjusted EPS narrowed to $2.03–$2.09 (from $1.98–$2.10), organic net sales were raised to -2.0% to -0.5% (from -3.5% to -1.5%), but constant-currency adjusted operating income was trimmed to -18% to -16% (from -18% to -14%) as incremental 2026 investment rose another $100 million to roughly $700 million. Sales guidance up, profit guidance down: the reinvestment is real, and it is being funded from the P&L. One caution into Q3: about 80 basis points of the quarter's sales came from inventory pull-forward that reverses next quarter, and management flagged phasing disruption ahead. Consensus expects Q3 EPS of $0.43, down from $0.47 at the time of the Q2 print and well below the $0.61 of a year ago.

2. The turnaround: new CEO, a paused split, and $700M of reinvestment

Cahillane took over January 1, 2026 · split paused February 2026 · 2027 is the proof year

Steve Cahillane, the former Kellanova CEO who led the Kellogg split, took over as CEO on January 1, 2026. Within weeks he paused the September-2025 plan to split Kraft Heinz into two listed companies — a roughly $15.4 billion "Global Taste Elevation" business (Heinz, Kraft Mac & Cheese, Philadelphia) and a roughly $10.4 billion North American grocery business (Oscar Mayer, Lunchables, Kraft Singles) — saying the company was not yet healthy enough to stand alone as two separate entities after a 5% volume and mix decline in the second half of 2025. The pause saves about $300 million in 2026 separation dis-synergies, redirected into a brand-reinvestment program that was lifted from $600 million to $700 million at the second quarter. The company is hiring in marketing and sales while cutting roughly 1,000 other roles this year, and 2026 capital expenditure is guided to about $950 million versus $801 million last year.

The reinvestment targets a genuine underinvestment gap: prior analysis had flagged Kraft Heinz's combined R&D and marketing spend at only 2–3% of sales, against mid-to-high single digits at peers. Early signs are directionally positive: the company has now beaten revenue expectations two quarters in a row after missing in eight of the prior nine, and the pace of market-share loss roughly halved, to 30 basis points in the first half of 2026 from 90 basis points a year earlier. But share is still being lost, and one brand explains most of it: Oscar Mayer drove about 60% of first-half share losses after its Deli Fresh lunch-meat packaging failed to reseal, costing distribution and sales. Redesigned packaging began rolling out in early August, and the CEO said in September that declines are lessening and sales per point of distribution are growing — worth watching, not yet bankable.

Innovation is finally pointed at where the consumer is going. PowerMac, launched in April, is a protein-enhanced mac & cheese (17 grams of protein, 6 grams of fiber, $2.99) explicitly marketed to consumers trying to maintain muscle while losing weight — a direct answer to GLP-1 appetite suppression. Capri Sun Hydrate is rolling out nationally with electrolytes and 50% less sugar, Lunchables got reduced-sugar updates, and the company signed Disney character licensing for its kids' brands and a five-year NFL global condiment partnership. Two straight beats and a halved share-loss pace are progress, not proof. Consensus expects organic growth of just 0.4% in 2027; one bull-side house expects 0.9%. The entire 2026 reset exists to make 2027 the year volumes turn — that is the underwriting question for this name.

3. Capital allocation: a 7.2% yield in preservation mode

$0.40 quarterly dividend · unchanged since the 2019 cut · leverage drifting to 3.3x

The dividend is the headline and the warning in one number: $0.40 per quarter, $1.60 annualized, yielding 7.21% at the current price — but it has not been raised since the February 2019 cut from $0.625, more than seven years ago. At roughly 78% of 2026 consensus adjusted EPS of $2.06 and about 51% of fiscal 2025 free cash flow of $3.7 billion, the payout is adequately covered by cash flow while looking stretched against earnings. GAAP coverage is meaningless given impairment-driven negative earnings. Read plainly: this is a dividend in preservation mode, priced as such, with no growth to underwrite. The last declared dividend was paid September 25, 2026.

Buybacks are effectively on ice. The $3 billion authorization runs through December 26, 2026; about $1.5 billion has been executed (45.5 million shares, or 3.77% of the company), with no repurchases in the first quarter of 2026 and no announced renewal. Leverage is moving the wrong way in the short term: net debt to EBITDA was 3.0x at the end of 2025 and is guided to no more than 3.3x in 2026, with a path back to roughly 3.0x in about two years. Gross debt stands at $19.0 billion against $2.68 billion of cash. The second quarter did show genuine deleveraging — $2.9 billion of debt repaid, funded in part by euro issuance used to tender longer, higher-coupon notes — and first-half free cash flow of $1.7 billion converted at 123%. Management's stated priorities are "sustaining the dividend and protecting our investment-grade credit profile," which is the right order of operations for a 3.3x-levered turnaround with a 7% yield to defend.

4. The Berkshire overhang

27.5% ownership · registered for sale, not yet sold · 3G fully exited in 2023

Berkshire Hathaway owns 325.4 million shares — 27.5% of Kraft Heinz, worth roughly $7.7 billion — and in January 2026 the company filed a prospectus supplement registering Berkshire's entire stake for potential sale, giving Berkshire the option to dispose of it at any time. The stock fell 4.9% on the news. Berkshire did not trim the position in the first or second quarter of 2026, and the stake was its eleventh-largest holding at about 2.6% of portfolio assets at mid-year. CEO Greg Abel called the investment "disappointing" and "well short of adequate" in his shareholder letter while adding that he has no immediate plans to sell following the pause of the corporate split. 3G Capital, the other legacy sponsor, fully exited its Kraft Heinz shares in 2023 and has no remaining involvement. Berkshire also took a $3.8 billion writedown on the position in the summer of 2025.

The overhang is live until a 13F shows reduction. A registered 27.5% stake held by an owner who has publicly called the investment disappointing is a ceiling on the multiple no earnings beat can fully lift. Any hint of trimming in the mid-November Q3 13F filing would likely pressure the stock regardless of fundamentals — and a disorderly overhang is exactly why the recommended entry sits 10% below the current price. Treat unverified claims that Berkshire has already begun selling as rumor: the documented 13F evidence shows no sale through the second quarter of 2026.

6. Thesis grid: why it could work, why it might not

Underwrite the cash flow; respect the volumes

Why it could work

A ~14% free-cash-flow yield with 123% conversion funds the dividend, the $700 million reinvestment, and deleveraging simultaneously. Two straight revenue beats after eight misses in nine quarters suggest the bleeding is slowing. The new CEO has run this exact playbook before at Kellanova. Share-loss pace halved, and the Oscar Mayer packaging fix has an early-August rollout with reported improvement. Emerging markets grew 8.5% organically with positive volume. The 7.2% yield is covered roughly two-to-one by free cash flow.

Why it might not

Five straight years of volume contraction and nine consecutive quarterly sales declines through 2025 are a habit, not a blip. North America volume and mix was still -3.8 points in Q2 despite pricing. Leverage is rising to 3.3x with $19 billion of gross debt. Berkshire's registered 27.5% stake is a live ceiling on the stock. Input inflation is expected at 4–5% in 2027 against a consumer already trading down to private label. The dividend has not grown in seven years, and GLP-1 appetite suppression structurally pressures the category.

9. Risks and catalysts into year-end

The next six weeks carry the whole thesis

Risks: (1) Private-label trade-down — inflation-weary shoppers keep migrating to store brands, with an incremental SNAP benefit-reduction headwind of about 100 basis points on 2026 organic sales. (2) Volume elasticity — pricing (+1.1 points in North America) is not covering volume loss (-3.8 points); five years of contraction is the core risk. (3) Input inflation — expected slightly above 4% in 2026 and 4–5% in 2027, with 2026 hedges rolling off. (4) GLP-1 appetite suppression — structurally pressuring processed snacks and meals; PowerMac is the counter but unproven at scale. (5) Balance sheet — $19 billion of gross debt against $2.68 billion of cash, with dividends, reinvestment, and capex all competing with deleveraging. (6) Regulation — the federal "Make America Healthy Again" push to phase out petroleum-based synthetic dyes spotlights Kraft Heinz's brightly colored shelf-stable products. (7) Execution — the entire bull case hinges on $700 million of reinvestment converting to volume growth in 2027; second-quarter operating-income guidance was already trimmed.
