1. Executive Summary & Investment Thesis

Southern Company is a textbook regulated compounder firing on every cylinder that matters: adjusted EPS of $1.13 in Q2 2026, up 23% year-on-year and $0.13 ahead of estimates, first-half adjusted EPS of $2.46 tracking at or near the top of the $4.50–$4.60 full-year guidance range, weather-adjusted retail sales growth of 2.3%, a 17-gigawatt contracted large-load pipeline, data-center load up ~49% year-on-year to over 1.2 GW, and residential rates in Georgia and Alabama held stable through 2029. The Vogtle nuclear overhang that defined this stock for a decade is over — both units are operating and earning in rate base.

And yet the stock sits near a 52-week low at $82.23, down ~12% over twelve months, because utilities are priced as bond proxies and the 10-year Treasury at 5.18% — after a September Fed hike, with more tightening priced — has compressed the whole sector. The street's consensus targets (~$100 average) imply ~21% upside but the consensus rating is only Hold: those targets are stale artifacts of a lower-rate regime, and the market is telling you the derating is real.

Bottom line: Initiate at BUY with a $92 12-month target — ~12% price upside plus a ~3.7% forward dividend yield for a ~16% total return. The target blends a dividend-discount value (~$87, using the $3.04 forward dividend at a 5% growth rate and 8.5% cost of equity) with a regulated P/E cross-check (~$93 at 19× estimated FY2027 EPS of $4.90), deliberately haircut from stale street targets for a 5%-plus rate world. The dislocation — a best-in-class regulated grower trading like a distressed bond — is the opportunity: earnings compound at 7–8% regardless of what the 10-year does.
Portfolio note: At 1.84% (~$31,363) SO is the portfolio's utility anchor and a high-quality income position, up sharply on a tiny adjusted legacy cost basis. The ~$1,159/year forward dividend stream (at the $0.76 quarterly rate) is secure and growing. This is the rare moment when a defensive compounder goes on sale for macro reasons unrelated to its earnings — the standing discipline is to add selectively toward ~2.5% on rate-driven weakness, not to chase.

4. Segment Analysis

Georgia Power — the growth engine

Net income of $779M, up 28% year-on-year (reported) — the quarter's standout. Georgia Power is where Vogtle Units 3 and 4 now earn in rate base, where the data-center pipeline is concentrated, and where ~10 GW of new generation projects are underway. It is also where the regulatory relationship matters most: the Georgia Public Service Commission's constructive stance (rate stability through 2029, large-load contract framework) underwrites the growth plan.

Alabama Power — the steady compounder

Net income of $437M, up 15% (reported). Alabama Power is the quiet quality story — formulaic rate mechanisms, supportive regulation, and industrial load growth. It rarely surprises and rarely disappoints, which is exactly what you want from ~20% of the earnings base.

Southern Company Gas — the diversifier

Net income of $126M, up 19% (reported), including a small state tax-refund benefit tied to a 2018 disposition. The gas LDCs (notably Nicor in Illinois and Atlanta Gas Light) add geographic and regulatory diversification plus their own pipe-replacement capex programs — rate-base growth outside the electric footprint.

Southern Power — the drag

A −$25M reported loss versus +$51M a year earlier, driven by accelerated depreciation from repowering projects and other excluded items. Southern Power is the competitive wholesale arm — contracted solar and gas generation sold into wholesale markets — and it is structurally the weakest link: no regulated return, exposure to power-price and recontracting risk. It is small enough (~5% of earnings in a normal year) not to break the thesis, but it is the reason the consolidated print always carries an asterisk.

5. Rate Base & the Capital Plan

For a regulated utility, the capital plan is the earnings plan: approved capex becomes rate base, rate base earns the allowed return on equity, and the allowed ROE flows through to EPS. Southern's plan is among the largest in the US utility sector.

  • Scale: ~10 GW of new generation projects underway across the system, plus transmission and grid-hardening investment — a multi-year, tens-of-billions capex program supporting ~7% annual rate-base growth (estimated from company guidance trajectory).
  • Vogtle done: Units 3 and 4 — the first new US nuclear units in 30+ years — are operating and in rate base. The decade of construction risk, cost overruns, and earnings charges is over; what remains is a 60–80-year stream of allowed returns on ~$30B+ of invested capital.
  • Financing discipline: a $700M equity raise in Q2 (reported) reduced the 2030 equity need to just $1.1B — meaning the bulk of the equity funding for the plan is already done, limiting future dilution. Management targets 17% FFO-to-debt, the credit-metric anchor that protects the investment-grade ratings underpinning cheap debt financing.
  • Kemper closed out: dismantlement of the abandoned gasifier assets was completed at end-2025 and site restoration is substantially complete — the last legacy construction disaster is now a footnote.

Rate-base growth of ~7% with an allowed ROE in the 10.5–11.5% range (typical for SO's jurisdictions) mechanically supports high-single-digit EPS growth before dilution — the arithmetic behind the 7–8% long-term EPS growth outlook.

6. Regulatory Environment: Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi

Regulation is the moat, and Southern's jurisdictions are among the most constructive in the country.

Georgia: the Georgia PSC has approved a framework in which residential rates are held stable through 2029 at the two largest subsidiaries while large-load (data-center) contracts cover 100% of their incremental costs — meaning hyperscaler demand funds its own infrastructure without burdening residential customers. The contracts carry ~$21B in collateral protections (reported on the Q2 call), a crucial mitigant against data-center counterparty risk. This is the gold-standard regulatory deal for the AI-load era: growth without ratepayer backlash.

Alabama: formulaic rate mechanisms with timely cost recovery and a historically supportive commission — the reason Alabama Power compounds quietly. No major rate-case drama on the horizon.

Mississippi: smaller and historically more contentious (Kemper), but the gasifier dismantlement is complete and the forward agenda is standard rate-base investment, not mega-project risk.

The regulatory risk that matters: data-center load is politically sensitive — if large-load growth ever does pressure residential rates, commissions can turn. Southern's 100%-incremental-cost contract structure and $21B of collateral are designed precisely for this, but the protection is contractual, not constitutional. Watch Georgia PSC proceedings for any shift in tone.

9. Competitive Position

Southern competes in the regulated-utility peer group (Duke, Dominion, NextEra's Florida utilities, AEP) where the differentiators are regulatory constructiveness, load growth, and execution on large capex. Southern screens best-in-class on all three: the Southeast has the strongest load-growth outlook in the US (manufacturing onshoring + data centers + population migration), Georgia/Alabama regulation is top-tier, and Vogtle — however painful — proved the company can execute first-of-a-kind nuclear construction. NextEra has the premium multiple and the renewables engine; Southern has the superior load-growth runway and now trades at a meaningful discount to it. Against Duke and Dominion, Southern's data-center pipeline and rate-stability framework are outright superior. The discount to peers is a rate-driven sector derating, not a company-specific discount — which is precisely why it is buyable.

10. Risks

  • Interest rates — the dominant risk. Utilities trade as bond proxies; with the 10-year at 5.18% and the Fed hiking, further rate rises compress the sector multiple regardless of earnings. This is already happening — it is why the stock is at a 52-week low. A 10-year at 6% would likely take the stock lower even on perfect execution.
  • Regulatory reversal on large load. If data-center growth pressures residential rates or reliability, commissions can rewrite the rules. The $21B collateral and 100%-cost-coverage contracts mitigate but do not eliminate this.
  • Data-center counterparty/conversion risk. 17 GW contracted is not 17 GW built — hyperscaler capex cycles, AI-demand disappointments, or project delays would slow the capex-to-rate-base flywheel.
  • Interest expense creep. Higher-for-longer raises the cost of the large debt-funded capex program; each 100bp on refinanced debt is a direct EPS headwind partially offsetting rate-base growth.
  • Southern Power drag. The competitive wholesale arm remains structurally weaker; further repowering charges or weak recontracting would keep the asterisk on consolidated results.
  • Storm exposure. Gulf Coast hurricanes are an annual wildcard — the utilities have storm-recovery mechanisms, but major events mean large upfront costs and regulatory lag.
  • Execution on 10 GW of new generation. The capex plan is the earnings plan; cost overruns or delays directly reduce earned returns.

Appendix B — Glossary

Rate base — the approved value of utility assets on which regulators allow a return. Allowed ROE — the return on equity regulators permit. AFUDC — allowance for funds used during construction; capitalized financing costs that boost reported earnings during build phases. FFO to debt — funds from operations divided by total debt; key credit metric. LDC — local distribution company (gas utility). IRP — integrated resource plan. GW — gigawatt.
