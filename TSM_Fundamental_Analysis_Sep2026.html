1. History, business model, and revenue mix

The short history

TSMC invented the pure-play foundry model in 1987 — the idea that a company could manufacture chips designed by others and never compete with its own customers — and has never been meaningfully challenged for the title of best-in-class since. Founder Morris Chang built the model on a simple insight: fabless chip design was becoming the industry's dominant structure, and someone needed to build the world-class factories that fabless companies would never build themselves. Chairman and CEO C.C. Wei now runs the company; under his leadership TSMC has executed the 7nm, 5nm and 3nm transitions essentially without a competitive stumble, while Samsung and Intel each lost at least one node generation to yield problems.

The last strategic chapter was geographic, not technological: under sustained pressure from Washington and its own customers, TSMC committed to a US manufacturing footprint that has grown from an initial $12B Arizona plan to $265B committed across what will eventually be multiple fabs, packaging facilities and an R&D center — announced in stages, most recently an additional $100B in July 2026. That chapter is still being written; Arizona's second fab (3nm) is targeted for volume production in 2H 2027, several quarters ahead of the original schedule, a rare acceleration for a first-of-its-kind overseas advanced-node build.

Business model

TSMC converts capital into cash in the most literal way possible: it builds fabs costing $15-20B+ apiece, fills them with EUV lithography tools bought from ASML (also in your book) at ~$200-400M each, and sells the output — wafers — to whichever company can pay for leading-edge capacity, which today means Nvidia, Apple, AMD, Broadcom and a widening circle of hyperscaler custom-silicon teams (Google TPU, Amazon Trainium, OpenAI's first TSMC-fabbed chip due late 2026). The economics work because TSMC is one of only three companies on earth that can manufacture at the leading edge, and the best of the three by a wide and currently widening margin. Customers do not switch mid-node — a chip taped out for TSMC's N3 cannot simply move to Samsung's SF3 — so once a customer commits to a node, TSMC effectively owns that customer's volume for the node's multi-year life.

Five years ago TSMC was a smartphone-and-PC foundry with a growing HPC side business. Today it is an AI-accelerator foundry that still happens to make iPhone chips. That shift is not a forecast — it has already happened, twice as fast as most sell-side models assumed as recently as 2023.

2. Moat and competitive landscape

Where the moat is real

  • A decisive, disclosed yield lead at the leading edge. Independent analyst tracking (KeyBanc, July 2025) put TSMC's N2 (2nm-class) die yield at roughly 65%, against Intel's 18A at ~55% and Samsung's SF2 at ~40%. For a large AI-accelerator die, that yield gap alone can move landed cost per good die by hundreds of dollars — it is the difference between a profitable wafer and a marginal one.
  • CoWoS advanced packaging is a second, compounding moat on top of the logic moat. An AI accelerator needs HBM stacked next to logic via CoWoS, and TSMC's CoWoS lines are, in CEO C.C. Wei's own words at the June 2026 shareholder meeting, "extremely tight and sold out through 2026," with independent trackers citing 52-78 week lead times and bookings extending into 2027. A fabless customer that wins TSMC wafer allocation still cannot ship without TSMC packaging — a double chokepoint.
  • The ecosystem is the deepest in the industry. 15 customers are already committed for 2nm, versus a far shorter list for Samsung or Intel's external foundry business. Design tools, IP libraries and process design kits are built around TSMC's node cadence first.

Where the moat is thinner than the bulls admit

  • Geography is becoming a real second axis of competition, independent of yield. Samsung's Taylor, Texas facility is slated to begin 2nm-class (SF2P+) production in 2027 — entirely outside the Taiwan risk perimeter. For AI programs increasingly subject to US export-control frameworks or federal supply-chain preferences, "made outside Taiwan" is becoming a feature customers will pay a yield discount for, not just a cost center. One trade-press report (unconfirmed against a signed volume commitment) claims Samsung's SF2P reached ~70% yield by January 2026 and is in negotiations with Anthropic and Meta for MTIA-3 production — if either converts to a signed contract, it would be the first credible evidence that a hyperscaler-scale AI buyer sees Samsung as more than a backup option. Treat this as a signal to watch, not a confirmed loss of share.
  • Customer concentration has quietly become extreme. Nvidia alone is now estimated at 22-25% of TSMC revenue, Apple a further ~18%, and the top five customers together an estimated 65-70% of revenue. TSMC's fortunes are now a leveraged bet on a handful of AI-accelerator and smartphone franchises staying healthy — not on the broad 500+ customer base the headline count implies.
  • The overseas build is a real, disclosed margin drag, not a rounding error. Management has stated plainly that overseas fab ramp-up will dilute companywide gross margin by 2-3 points in early stages, widening to 3-4 points as those fabs mature — layered on top of a separate 3-4 point drag already guided from the N2 ramp itself in H2 2026. This is the cost of the geopolitical insurance policy described above, and it is being paid in real margin dollars today, not deferred to a footnote.

The valuation gap is the most interesting fact in this section. TSMC trades at a lower trailing multiple than the AI-accelerator designers it manufactures for (NVDA 29.1x, AVGO 45.7x) and than its own equipment supplier (ASML 53.3x) — despite arguably having the deepest and most durable moat of the four. That gap is not obviously irrational: TSMC is far more capital-intensive (see §4) and carries a geopolitical risk premium the fabless names largely avoid. But it means the "AI infrastructure is overvalued" narrative, if true, is least true at TSMC.

3. Growth drivers

Driver 1 — AI accelerator demand (proven, compounding, and re-accelerating)

This is no longer a forecast; it is the largest line in the business. HPC revenue grew 20% quarter-over-quarter in Q2 2026 alone to reach 66% of total revenue. Management raised its long-term AI-accelerator revenue CAGR assumption from 50% to 56-59% at the Q1 2026 call, and then raised full-year 2026 total-company revenue growth guidance to "slightly above 40%" in USD terms at the Q2 call — a guidance raise, not a guidance hold, delivered alongside an $8B capex increase in the same quarter. Management also flagged a structural broadening within AI itself: the rise of "agentic AI" is reviving CPU demand inside AI data centers alongside the GPU/XPU accelerators, adding a second silicon-demand vector on top of the original accelerator thesis.

Driver 2 — Advanced packaging (CoWoS) as a second, newer moat (proven, capacity-constrained)

CoWoS revenue reached $9.6B in 2025 — 2.5x the older InFO packaging platform Apple pioneered — driven by Nvidia and AMD. Capacity is sold out through 2026 and bookings already extend into 2027. TSMC is guiding roughly 10-20% of its 2026 capital budget directly at packaging, testing and mask capacity, on top of the 70-80% going to advanced logic nodes.

Driver 3 — 2nm (N2) and the roadmap beyond it (proven, near-term margin cost)

N2 entered high-volume production in Q4 2025 with what management describes as solid yield, and reached 3% of wafer revenue in Q2 2026, already growing sequentially. The follow-on N2P (backside power delivery, 2026) and A16/A14 (angstrom-era, 2027-2028) roadmap is TSMC's mechanism for staying a full generation ahead of Samsung's SF2Z (which does not add backside power until 2027). The near-term cost of this leadership is real: N2 ramp is itself guided to dilute gross margin 2-3 points in 2026 (see §2).

Driver 4 — Reshoring / geopolitical-insurance capacity (optionality on returns, real on cost)

The $265B Arizona commitment, plus expansion in Japan (Kumamoto, second fab targeting 2028) and continuing Taiwan capacity, functions as much as a hedge against export-control and Taiwan-risk disruption as it does as growth capacity. Arizona's first fab (Fab 21 Phase 1) has already turned a real, if modest, profit — roughly $514M in its first full year, and TSMC Arizona's 1H 2026 profit was up 663% year-over-year off that low base — evidence the overseas bet is not purely dilutive. I assign this driver zero incremental revenue upside in the base case (it largely re-locates capacity TSMC would have built in Taiwan anyway) and treat it purely as a margin cost and a risk-mitigant, which is the more conservative and, I think, more honest framing.

Owner earnings / cash-flow quality: the gross-margin sensitivity that actually matters here

TSMC's depreciation policy (5-year schedules for fab equipment) is not the contested question it is at, say, a hyperscaler depreciating GPUs — TSMC's own equipment life assumptions are broadly in line with actual replacement cadence, and management does not appear to be flattering earnings through an aggressive useful-life assumption. The genuine uncertainty here is how much of the current ~66% gross margin survives the disclosed dilution path from overseas fabs (2-3pp early, widening to 3-4pp later) stacked on N2 ramp costs (a further 2-4pp through 2026). Management's own stated through-cycle target is "56% and higher."

Red flag worth its own paragraph: the concentration in this portfolio is deeper than the position size suggests.

At 0.45% of the portfolio, TSM looks like a small, diversifying satellite position. It is not. TSMC manufactures the actual silicon behind both NVDA and AVGO — two positions already sized at meaningful weights in this book — and its sole EUV lithography supplier is ASML, also held directly. A single adverse event in the Taiwan Strait, or even a sharp AI-capex digestion cycle, would not diversify across NVDA/AVGO/ASML/TSM/GRID/GEV/SOXQ; it would hit all seven simultaneously, because they are the same underlying bet expressed through seven tickers. The position size is small; the correlation is not. This is worth stating plainly because a 0.45% line item is easy to wave through on the "it's tiny" heuristic — the heuristic doesn't hold when the tiny position sits at the structural center of six other, larger positions.

7. Valuation and five-year outlook

The setup. At $428.64 — 18% below the June 30, 2026 all-time high of $479 and roughly back to where the stock traded in mid-July, before the Q2 print — you are being offered TSMC's cleanest trailing multiple (30.9x, no adjustment needed) against the strongest demand signal in the company's history (management has raised full-year 2026 revenue growth guidance twice in six months, most recently to "slightly above 40%"). The stock is also, on a trailing basis, cheaper than every AI-accelerator designer and equipment supplier it touches — NVDA, AVGO and ASML all trade at richer trailing multiples (§2).

What is priced in at $428.64?

Run it backwards: to earn a market 10%/yr for five years, TSM needs to reach roughly $690 by 2031. At a conservative 20x exit multiple — well below where TSM trades today (30.9x trailing) and below every peer in §2 — that requires 2031 EPS of just $34.52, an EPS CAGR of only 15.6%/yr from my 2026E base. Even at a genuinely punitive 16x exit multiple, the required CAGR is only 20.8%/yr — below the 27.4% EPS growth TSMC delivered in 2025 alone, and well below the 40%+ revenue growth (and higher EPS growth, given operating leverage) it just guided for 2026. The market is not asking you to underwrite a heroic acceleration from here — it is asking you to underwrite a deceleration to roughly half of TSMC's current growth rate, sustained for five years, without the exit multiple derating below where cheap, ex-growth semi-cap names already trade. That is a real ask, but it is a much lower bar than the "priced for perfection" framing this stock sometimes gets in the financial press.

Fit with your portfolio's growth objective

Yield is irrelevant here, as it is everywhere in this book — consistent with the pension-funded income floor, TSM should be judged purely on total return. Your 10-year horizon is a genuine advantage: the CoWoS/2nm capacity build is a multi-year story that will have real air pockets (the Q3 2026 gross-margin guide already shows sequential compression from N2 ramp costs), and a 10-year holder can look through a cyclical digestion phase that would force a trader out at the worst moment. But this is the most concentrated single name in your AI-capex theme, not a diversifying add to it. TSM manufactures your NVDA and AVGO chips and buys tools from your ASML position — a Taiwan Strait shock or a sharp hyperscaler capex pause would not diversify across those four names plus GRID, GEV and SOXQ; it would hit all seven at once. Size any further TSM add with that combined exposure in mind, not TSM's 0.45% weight in isolation. Tax location is a non-issue: TSM's sub-1% qualified-dividend-style distribution carries no meaningful tax-location preference, so the taxable account is fine.

The three things to actually watch

  1. Q3 2026 print (mid-October 2026). Guided at $44.6-45.8B revenue, 65-67% gross margin. The tell is whether gross margin holds near the top of that range (confirming the N2/overseas dilution is tracking to plan) or breaks toward the bottom (a signal the disclosed 2-4pp dilution range is proving optimistic).
  2. A signed hyperscaler volume commitment at Samsung's 2nm-class node. The Anthropic/Meta MTIA-3 reports remain unconfirmed as of this report. A signed contract would be the first hard evidence that TSMC's near-monopoly at the leading edge is genuinely eroding, not just narrowing on paper.
  3. Finalization of the Section 232 semiconductor tariff framework. Commerce Secretary Lutnick indicated as recently as September 2, 2026 that an exemption framework for US-based production is under development — TSMC's $265B Arizona commitment should qualify, but the scope and rate are not yet final.

Bottom line

TSMC is a wonderful business trading at a genuinely honest price. Unlike almost every other name in this book's AI-capex coverage, its trailing multiple contains no accounting noise to strip out, and its reverse-DCF math does not require heroic assumptions — a 10% return needs meaningfully less growth than the company is currently delivering, even against a conservative exit multiple. The moat is real and, at the leading edge, is still widening rather than narrowing, evidenced by a disclosed yield gap over Samsung and Intel that neither has closed despite years of trying.

My call is hold and continue to build gradually, not chase. The base case clears your 10%/yr hurdle with room to spare, and the bear case is a round-trip rather than a genuine capital-loss scenario over a 10-year horizon — an unusually favorable asymmetry for a stock trading near its all-time high. But the position's true size in this portfolio is not 0.45% — it is 0.45% plus the NVDA, AVGO, ASML, GRID, GEV and SOXQ positions that all move on the same underlying cycle, with a Taiwan Strait tail risk sitting underneath all seven simultaneously. Keep VXUS/AVUV redeployment ahead of any further TSM add in the cash queue, as established, and size the next add against that combined exposure rather than against TSM in isolation.
