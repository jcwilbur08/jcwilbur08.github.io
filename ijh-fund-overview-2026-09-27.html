1. Executive Summary & Verdict

IJH is the default mid-cap index vehicle for a reason: it tracks the S&P MidCap 400 — the only major U.S. mid-cap index whose members must pass a profitability test (positive trailing four-quarter earnings and a positive most recent quarter) before a committee ever admits them. That screen systematically keeps the unprofitable tail out of the portfolio, which is the structural reason the S&P 400 has historically compounded at a premium to the rules-based Russell Midcap alternative. At 0.05% expense ratio, roughly $121 billion in assets, and a 26-year operating history since its May 2000 inception, IJH is the largest and most liquid mid-cap ETF in existence.

2026 has been a mixed year for the mid-cap story. IJH's NAV total return is about +10.3% year-to-date through mid-September (reported) — solid, but trailing the Russell Midcap index, which has run hotter this year on large-cap-graduates and a deeper value rotation. Over longer windows the quality screen has earned its keep, and tracking has been surgical: the fund's multi-year NAV returns sit within a few basis points of the index (reported), exactly what a 0.05% fee predicts.

Bottom line — verdict: KEEP. IJH is a best-in-class mid-cap index fund whose profitability screen is a genuine structural advantage. It stays as the mid-cap vehicle in its account: the portfolio-level consolidation originally sketched here is unavailable — IJH and FSMDX sit in different retirement accounts. At 1.17% and up 325% on a $17.11 cost basis, IJH is a long-term winner that has earned its place. Action: none.

The honest keep-one-or-both answer

Start with what is not in dispute: these two funds are economic near-duplicates. Both are mid-cap blend index funds, both live in the same Morningstar category, and their returns move together — the second fund adds almost no diversification the first doesn't already provide. Holding both is belt-and-suspenders on a 3% sleeve. If Cam wants the mid-cap exposure simplified to a single line, that is the right instinct.

The decision between them, honestly weighed:

  • IJH's case — the profitability screen. The S&P 400's earnings requirement is the only structural difference that matters, and it is a genuine one: it systematically excludes the unprofitable tail that the Russell Midcap admits. Over full cycles this quality filter has produced a persistent premium — and it is why the S&P 400, not the Russell Midcap, is the benchmark serious mid-cap managers measure themselves against. IJH is also twice the assets, 26 years old, and the most liquid mid-cap vehicle in the world.
  • FSMDX's case — cost and breadth. Half the fee (0.025% vs 0.05%), 805 names vs 396, and a 4-star Morningstar rating vs IJH's 3-star. Its 2026 outperformance is real — but it comes partly from near-large-cap constituents (AMD, Newmont) that are arguably not mid-cap exposure at all, and partly from the year's value rotation. Neither is a structural edge.
  • The fee gap is a rounding error. 2.5 basis points on $30.5K is ~$7.60/year. It should carry zero weight in the decision.
  • The tax angle favors keeping IJH. In a taxable account, consolidating means selling one fund: FSMDX carries an ~11% embedded gain (~$3,045, calculated), IJH carries a +325% embedded gain (~$15,120, calculated). Selling FSMDX realizes far less gain. In a retirement account the direction is frictionless either way — confirm the account registration before executing.
Portfolio recommendation: KEEP IJH as the mid-cap index vehicle in its account — the profitability screen is the differentiator worth paying 2.5 basis points for. FSMDX stays in its own account; the two cannot be merged across retirement accounts, so the cross-account overlap stands as a venial redundancy rather than an actionable consolidation. The survivor debate is settled by the account structure, not by the fee comparison.

8. Risks / What Could Change the Verdict

  • Mid-cap cyclicality: mid-caps are full equity risk with a domestic-cyclical tilt (industrials ~20%) — the −13.1% 2022 print is the template for what a recession does to this sleeve. There is no defensive cushion here.
  • Committee risk: the S&P 400's committee selection is a feature (quality screen) and a risk (discretion). A committee can be slow to remove deteriorating names or add emerging ones relative to a rules-based index.
  • Style drift at the edges: successful mid-caps graduate to the S&P 500 and are removed; the index perpetually restocks with smaller, earlier-stage names — a built-in "sell your winners" dynamic that is a drag in momentum regimes.
  • Size-creep competition: the Russell Midcap's larger constituents (AMD-scale names) mean FSMDX will periodically outrun IJH in large-cap-led rallies — expect the comparison to look unfavorable in exactly those years. Judge the screen over cycles, not years.
  • Redundancy drag: keeping both IJH and FSMDX permanently means two expense ratios, two tax lots, and two lines on the statement for one exposure. Not a return risk — an attention risk.
  • What would change the verdict to REPLACE: a fee hike, persistent multi-year tracking failure, or S&P weakening the profitability requirement — the screen is the thesis, and without it IJH is just a more expensive FSMDX.

Appendix B — Glossary

TTM — trailing twelve months. NAV total return — return including reinvested distributions, measured at net asset value. Tracking difference — fund return minus index return over a period (distinct from tracking error, its volatility). Float-adjusted — index weighting based on shares available to public investors. Hybrid sampling — holding a representative subset of index securities rather than all of them. Mid-cap blend — Morningstar category for mid-cap funds mixing growth and value styles.
