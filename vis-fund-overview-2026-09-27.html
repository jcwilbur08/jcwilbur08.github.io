1. Executive Summary & Verdict

VIS is Vanguard’s broad US industrials index fund: ~400 holdings (reported) across large, mid, and small caps, tracking the MSCI US Investable Market Industrials 25/50 Index, at a 0.09% expense ratio (reported) that undercuts the industrials-fund average by more than tenfold. It is, on the merits, an excellent vehicle — the cheapest way to own the full US industrial economy in one line, with tight index tracking and a ~10.9% annualized return since its 2004 inception. The three-star Morningstar fund rating is average, but that score measures backward-looking risk-adjusted returns against peers, not the quality of the vehicle itself.

The issue is not the fund. It is the size. Sixteen shares — 0.31% of the portfolio (calculated), ~$5,232 — is a toe-dip that rounds to zero in portfolio terms. It does not diversify the book, it does not hedge the industrial single names, and it does not earn a seat on any committee. A starter position only makes sense if there is a standing plan to make it a position.

Verdict: KEEP — with a build-or-exit decision. The vehicle is sound and there is no reason to sell a quality fund at a 5.7% unrealized loss for a ~$314 haircut. But 0.31% earns nothing: either build it toward ~1% as the portfolio’s broad-industrials sleeve (adding ~35 shares on a pullback toward ~$300 would do it) or consolidate the ~$5,200 away into the GRID add line or GEV. A starter without a plan is dead money by another name.

2. Objective & Index Tracked

VIS seeks to track the investment performance of the MSCI US Investable Market Industrials 25/50 Index, a benchmark of large-, mid-, and small-capitalization US stocks in the Industrials sector as defined by the Global Industry Classification Standard (GICS). The index covers the full industrial economy: aerospace and defense, construction and engineering, electrical equipment, industrial machinery, commercial services, and transportation. The fund is passively managed, advised by The Vanguard Group through its Quantitative Equity Group, and has been operating since September 23, 2004.

The “25/50” in the index name is the important detail: it is a capping methodology that constrains any single issuer to 25% and the sum of all issuers above 5% to 50% of the index. In practice this keeps the fund diversified across ~400 names and prevents the mega-caps (Caterpillar, GE Aerospace) from overwhelming the basket — the structural reason VIS is a genuine sector proxy rather than a disguised large-cap fund.

8. Risks / What Could Change the Verdict

  • Cyclical beta. Industrials are economically sensitive: a downturn hits orders, backlogs, and multiples simultaneously. Beta of ~1.04–1.07 (reported) understates the drawdown in a real recession for the machinery and transportation names.
  • Aerospace/defense concentration. Nearly a quarter of the fund (23.6%, reported) is aerospace and defense — Boeing, RTX, Lockheed, GE Aerospace. A defense-budget or commercial-aviation shock is a sector-level event for this fund.
  • Uber classification quirk. At ~2.2%, a rideshare company rides along in an “industrials” fund. Harmless at this weight, but it shows GICS sector definitions are not always intuitive.
  • Lower AUM and volume than XLI. ~$8.5B in assets and ~200K average daily volume (reported) are ample for retail-sized trades but thinner than XLI’s ~$31B — wider spreads on large block trades. Irrelevant at Cam’s size.
  • Average peer rating. The 3-star Morningstar fund rating reflects middling risk-adjusted returns against peers over the rating window — a reminder that sector beta, however cheap, is not alpha.
What would change the verdict: an expense-ratio increase or a change in Vanguard’s indexing approach would force a re-underwrite of the vehicle itself; a sustained industrials bear market would argue for the build path (cheaper entry toward $280–300) rather than the exit path. A drift above ~1.5% on strength would argue for trimming back. None of these conditions currently hold — the decision on the table is sizing, not quality.
