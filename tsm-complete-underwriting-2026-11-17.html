Section 01 · The desk call

Buy, 6/10, $450 — the DCF took the old target down

This is a genuine downgrade, not a trim. The reader asked for a DCF; the DCF answered, and its answer challenges both the $545 target and, at the provider's own discount rate, the current price. The desk is keeping the earnings-multiple case and conceding the discount-rate case — that combination is a Buy at $450, not a Strong Buy at $545.

Why the target falls

The $545 target was set on October 15 at ~24.4× FY2027E consensus EPS of $22.30. Two things have changed since. First, the discount-rate environment: the 10-year Treasury is ~5.12%, beta is 1.23, and a standard build-up lands at a ~10–11% WACC. On the desk's five-year FCF path, $545 requires a WACC of ~6.04% — pricing TSMC as if it carried almost no equity risk premium. Second, the 2027 capex surge: Deutsche Bank's $84B 2027 forecast holds free cash flow to ~$49B even as revenue grows 33%, so the cash-flow case cannot carry a 24× multiple either.

The new $450 target concedes that point directly. It is struck at 19.7× FY2027E EPS of $22.90 — roughly the stock's own average forward multiple of ~19.5× — and 15.8× FY2028E EPS of $28.50. It is a multiples target and is not DCF-supported. At the provider WACC the DCF says ~$259; the exit-multiple cross-check says $353.50. The desk is underwriting the earnings path and the moat, with eyes open about what the bond market does to the arithmetic.

Why conviction is 6, not 7

Four deductions, each documented in this report: the DCF challenge itself; a price 7.4% below its 200-day moving average with the tape still falling into the print; the Nvidia print tonight still unresolved, with the governance overhang running into December 1–3–10 congressional hearings; and the 2027 capex surge holding FCF to ~$49B. Against those: estimates rose, October was a record, Q4 tracks ahead, and Morningstar carries a $534 fair value at 5 stars. That balance is a 6.

DCF verdict on $545 — Challenge

Built on the named provider's WACC (10.84%), a stated FCF path anchored to consensus revenue and Deutsche capex, and 3% terminal growth, the DCF values TSM at ~$259 per ADR — 52% below $545 and 31% below the $373.45 reference. Why it fell — drivers, ranked by evidence

  1. The Nvidia overhang (largest TSMC-specific driver). Jensen Huang resigned Nov 12 (board accepted Nov 11). On Nov 16, reporting based on three sources said the board is not considering bringing him back, despite a 180,000-signature petition, public support from Elon Musk, and President Trump saying he "would not mind" a return; the board is focused on a conduct/national-security probe and a successor search. Late Nov 16, the Justice Department opened a criminal investigation into Megaspeed — reported second-hand, flagged as such in the appendix. NVDA fell 13% last week and a further 4.1% on Nov 16 to $156.14. TSMC is Nvidia's sole leading-edge foundry; a governance crisis at its largest AI customer is a direct demand-visibility risk, and ruling out a return removed the relief rally.
  2. New export controls. A BIS interim final rule on Nov 16 — "Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors" — requires licences for advanced AI chips shipped to Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand and Brazil, and for any data centre worldwide giving China/Macau/Country Group D:5-headquartered companies more than 12,000,000 aggregate TPP of remote compute. Effective on Federal Register publication, scheduled Nov 19, with a 90-day transition for existing data centres. TSMC's impact is second-order — it sells wafers, not chips — but the rule raises friction on the Singapore/Malaysia data-centre demand that feeds TSMC orders.
  3. December Fed-hike pricing. The 10-year Treasury sits at ~5.12%, up from 4.86% on Nov 6. Pricing for a December +25bp hike runs 90–96% across venues (CME 95%, LSEG 96%, Kalshi 90%) — venue-dependent, never one settled number. Long-duration growth de-rates mechanically in this tape; TSM's DCF is duration-heavy, as Section 04 shows.
  4. The broader AI selloff. The Nov 5 S&P 500 −4.3% day set the tone. Discussion Nov 13–16 centred on analyst demand-forecast cuts, short positioning in the semiconductor complex, rotation into staples, and doubts about supplier-financed AI demand. No TSMC-specific demand cut was published Nov 13–17.
  5. Memory — indirect. Record memory prices lift HBM/CoWoS demand for TSMC but tax its smartphone and PC customers and inflate hyperscaler build costs. Two-sided; covered in Section 08.

TSMC's own news, Nov 13–17 — nothing material to demand

The freshest company news remains Nov 10: the record October revenue, ~$40.93B of board-approved capital appropriations (including 2027 R&D capex), the Q3 dividend raised to NT$9.00 (+28.6%), and authorisations of up to $500m each for Anthropic IPO shares and OpenAI preferred stock — spending ceilings, not completed purchases. Nov 12 brought a Form 6-K on employee personal data appearing on an external website; TSMC says there is no evidence its own systems were compromised and the matter is unrelated to operations or customer data. On Nov 15–16, Bloomberg reported Nvidia sought TSMC's advice in a Taiwanese chip-diversion prosecution of an Nvidia manager; TSMC declined to get involved and declined to comment. Analyst action on TSM Nov 13–17: none found in the sources searched — stated as "none found," not a verified absence.

The Huang calendar — what is actually scheduled

House: closed-door transcribed interviews — Huang on Dec 1, General Counsel Tim Teter on Dec 2 — requested by the Foreign Affairs, China Select, and Intelligence chairs, with a subpoena threat if Huang had not confirmed by Nov 14. As of Nov 15, Huang had agreed and dates were still being discussed; a Nov 17 report states the Dec 1 appearance is confirmed. Senate Banking: public hearing with Huang on Dec 3; Commerce Secretary Lutnick on Dec 10. An earlier report of a closed-door briefing "before Thanksgiving" conflicts with the Dec 1 date and is treated as superseded. No TSMC involvement is alleged in any source found. Separately, Senate and House Democrats opened a second front Nov 12–13 into the administration's approval and taxation of H200 exports to China.

The capex path — the model's swing factor

2026 guide: $60–64B (raised Jul 16 from $52–56B; reiterated Oct 15), with Q1–Q3 actuals of $40.64B implying a heavy $19.36–23.36B Q4. For 2027, Deutsche Bank (Oct 20) forecasts $84B, and $100B for 2028, against market consensus of $76B/$88B. Corroboration clusters high: Goldman Sachs $85B, UBS $90B, BofA $80–85B, Citi $81B for 2027. The base case uses Deutsche's $84B/$100B — the hawkish end — because it is the most recent named sell-side forecast and because using consensus $76B would flatter FCF. Management was more cautious on the Oct 15 call, citing component price rises and macro uncertainty; that tension is flagged. Every $10B of 2027 capex is ~$1.93/ADR of FCF in that year.

Five-year FCF projection (desk model — assumptions stated)

Method: FCF = operating cash flow − capex; OCF = consensus revenue × an assumed 58% OCF margin (below Q3's 61.6%, allowing for the 3–4pp overseas-fab dilution TSMC itself guides and for memory-cost pass-through lag). Capex plateaus at $95–100B after 2028 — the single most optimistic judgement in the model, and what lets FCF inflect in 2029.

What would change the verdict

Three things: a sustained fall in the 10-year yield toward ~4% (WACC ~9.5–10%); a capex plateau proven by TSMC's own January 2027 guidance rather than forecast by a bank; or FCF margins returning to the ~30% TTM level a year earlier than modelled. Until one of those lands, the gap is driven, in order, by (1) the discount rate, (2) the 2027–28 capex surge, (3) terminal growth.

Section 08 · The wider board

Demand, memory, competition, geopolitics

The demand is real and the backlogs are large — Microsoft commercial RPO $678B, Google Cloud backlog $514B, AWS ~$496B. But it is increasingly funded with debt and equity: ~$170B of hyperscaler bonds issued in 2026, an $85B Alphabet equity issue, hyperscaler FCF of −$40B in Q3 2026. That funding shift is exactly what the Nov 5–16 selloff is repricing — and it is the demand-side mirror of the DCF's discount-rate problem.

Memory — a two-sided tailwind and tax

Conventional DRAM contract prices rose +93–98% in Q1 2026, +58–63% in Q2, +13–18% in Q3, with +10–15% forecast for Q4. Q3 global DRAM revenue rose +369% YoY to ~$200B — on bit shipments up only 4% QoQ. The growth is price, not volume. Suppliers guide tightness through 2028–2030.

For TSMC the effect cuts both ways. Positive: HBM is the binding constraint on Rubin/Blackwell racks, so memory scarcity raises the value of TSMC's CoWoS capacity — every HBM stack must be packaged by TSMC — and supports the 2027 capex case. Negative: memory is now 30–40% of a smartphone's bill of materials (vs 10–15% a year ago), squeezing TSMC's handset and PC customers, and it inflates the data-centre build costs that compete with wafer orders for the same hyperscaler budgets. Net: a tailwind for TSMC's AI mix, a headwind for its non-AI mix and for customer affordability. A Nov 14 court ruling allowed a DRAM price-fixing class action to proceed past dismissal — the allegations are unproven and denied; response due Dec 7.

Custom silicon — diversification with a circularity wrinkle

Anthropic's Nov 10 filing amendment disclosed that seven suppliers agreed to buy $26B of Anthropic stock at the IPO price — Nvidia $10B, Google $10B, AMD $2.5B, Broadcom $1B, Micron $1B, Microsoft $1B, and TSMC $500m. Broadcom additionally stands behind $42B of lending to Anthropic and is arranging >$50B for the custom chips it is building with OpenAI. Anthropic will deploy 1 GW of Ironwood TPUs in 2026, 5 GW of the next generation in 2027, 10 GW in 2028, and is set to become Broadcom's largest XPU customer in 2027.

Every Broadcom/Google TPU and OpenAI inference accelerator is a TSMC wafer order — custom silicon diversifies TSMC away from Nvidia-only dependence, which matters more this week than it did last week. But TSMC is now an equity holder in two customers whose demand it helps finance, and the custom-silicon mix carries lower wafer prices than merchant GPUs. The circularity question is one the market started asking on Nov 5; it has not been answered.

Competition and the moat — intact

  • 2nm (N2): in volume production since Q4 2025; 8% of Q3 wafer revenue; reported yields 60–70%; capacity ~50–60k wafers/month in H1 2026, targeting 100k by end-2026 and 120k in early 2027. Order visibility stretches to 2028, with room seen for 5–10% advanced-process wafer price rises in 2027.
  • CoWoS: fully booked through 2027; capacity ~70k wafers/month at end-2025 → >130k by end-2026 → 200k targeted in 2027; demand still ~3× supply; Nvidia takes >60% of output. CoWoS, not logic wafers, remains the binding constraint on AI accelerator shipments.
  • Samsung: 2nm yield climbed to ~60% from ~30% in mid-2025 — still below the threshold major customers require; foundry share 6.8%, down from 9.3% YoY. A Samsung–Broadcom supply MOU (>$200B through 2030) is a watch item, not yet an order.
  • Intel: 18A yield reports conflict — 85% in one source, 55–75% in others in the same search set; treated as an unverified range, not a fact. Claimed design wins are single-sourced and unverified. Foundry losses of ~$7B a year and no proven external AI-scale customer remain the consensus read.
  • Arizona: $165B, six fabs; 2nm will not be produced there until 2028 at the earliest. Overseas fabs are guided to dilute gross margin by ~3–4pp over the next few years.

Net: no credible second source at the leading edge for 2026–28. Samsung and Intel are narrowing the yield gap at the margin; neither changes the underwriting.

Taiwan Strait — live, not theoretical

The PRC's unannounced exercise ran Nov 1–6: 100+ naval and coast-guard vessels at peak, simulating isolation of Taiwan from outside intervention, with a carrier group conducting area-denial drills. Roughly 70 vessels remained on station Nov 13, and the package was thinning in hull count by Nov 16–17 without giving back ground. G7 foreign ministers (Nov 10–11) called the activity east of Taiwan a threat to regional stability; Beijing objected, Taipei welcomed the statement. Watch Nov 19: the Trump–Xi meeting at APEC Shenzhen, with chips and AI export curbs on the agenda — whether the naval package eases or spikes around the meeting is the tell. The market currently prices the exercise as background risk, not the proximate driver: the TAIEX rose 1.35% on Nov 13 despite the drills and fell 6.03% on Nov 16 on the Nvidia/Fed tape. No TSMC operational disruption has been reported.

The December FOMC — the second discount-rate event

The FOMC meets Dec 8–9, decision Dec 9. Hike pricing across venues: 90–96% for +25bp (CME 95%, LSEG 96%, Kalshi 90%), with a 50bp hike priced at 4–6% everywhere — essentially priced out. One secondary source claiming "100% priced" is not corroborated by any venue and is disregarded. Yields: 10-year 5.11–5.13%, 2-year 4.81%, 30-year 5.47%. For TSM a hike is a double hit — a higher discount rate on a long-duration cash-flow stream (every 1pp of WACC is worth roughly $30–50/ADR at 3% terminal growth, per the sensitivity grid) and tighter funding costs for the hyperscalers who fund the order book.

Section 09 · Thesis, risks & what to watch

The bull case strengthened. The bear case is the discount rate.

Bull case — what strengthened since Nov 12

Operating momentum accelerated: October was the largest revenue month in company history and puts Q4 on pace for the top half of a guide raised three times in 2026. Consensus FY2027 EPS rose to $22.90, so the multiple case got cheaper as the price fell — 16.3× FY2027E, 13.1× FY2028E, against 33% revenue growth. The moat is intact: 2nm at 8% of wafer revenue, CoWoS sold out through 2027, no second source at the leading edge. Hyperscaler capex heads to ~$1T in 2027 on ~$1.7T of combined cloud backlogs. Morningstar moved to 5 stars at $534; the Street averages ~$586 with zero Sell ratings. The dividend rose 28.6% and the balance sheet holds ~$92–95B of net cash. On this reading, the selloff is macro and governance de-risking into a known catalyst — and the October print is the evidence.

Bear case — what weakened since Nov 12

The price action is Exhibit A: −7.9% in two sessions, 7.4% below the 200-day, RSI 38.7 and falling, with $353.10 the only nearby support and the tape still falling into the print. The Nvidia crisis is escalating into a Dec 1–3–10 congressional calendar, a DOJ criminal probe, and a new BIS rule aimed at the Singapore/Malaysia data-centre channel. The Fed is ~95% priced to hike with the 10-year above 5.1% — the exact environment that punishes a duration stock, and the desk's own DCF says ~$259 at the provider WACC: the market price still embeds a ~6–7% WACC the bond market does not support. The 2027 capex surge holds FCF to ~$49B, so "cheap on FY2027 earnings" is partly a capex-deferral argument. Memory hyperinflation taxes non-AI customers, and ~70 PRC vessels on station are a standing reminder that the Taiwan discount is not a historical artefact.

  3. Q4 print, ~mid-January 2027, and the 2027 capex signal — the January call is when TSMC confirms (or doesn't) a 2027 capex number in the $76–84B range. That single number moves the DCF's 2027 FCF by ~$2/ADR per $10B and is the most important fundamental input after tonight's guide.

Key risks, ranked

  1. Nvidia demand / governance shock

    A guide cut or Rubin delay tonight; escalation at the Dec 1–3–10 hearings; further BIS tightening. A direct hit to TSMC's largest AI order stream.

  2. Rates / Fed hawkishness

    A December hike (90–96% priced) plus a hawkish 2027 path. A 10-year above 5.25% re-rates the whole AI complex lower regardless of fundamentals.

  3. Taiwan Strait escalation

    The PRC exercise demonstrated isolation capability without announcement. A blockade or quarantine scenario is the one risk that is not diversifiable and not in any multiple.

  4. Capex / margin dilution

    2027 capex of $84–100B with 3–4pp of overseas-fab dilution. If the hoped-for 5–10% wafer price rises for 2027 do not land, margins compress while FCF troughs.

  5. Memory-cost squeeze on customers

    DRAM/NAND hyperinflation hitting handset and PC demand and hyperscaler build budgets — a demand air-pocket in non-AI segments.

  6. Custom-silicon share shift

    Broadcom/Google/OpenAI ASICs growing faster than merchant GPUs. TSMC still manufactures them, but at different wafer economics and with customer concentration shifting.

  7. Circular financing

    Supplier equity and debt funding of AI-lab demand (the $26B Anthropic supplier stock purchase; $42B of Broadcom lending). If lab funding tightens, the order book's quality gets questioned.
