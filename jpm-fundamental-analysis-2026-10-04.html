02 | Thesis

Investment thesis

1

The deposit franchise is the moat

JPMorgan serves roughly half of American households, with $2.69T in average deposits (+7% y/y) and 63.7M active mobile customers. Sticky, low-cost deposits fund the entire $5T balance sheet more cheaply than any rival can replicate — the structural advantage behind every point of excess return on tangible equity.

2

The CIB is the cyclical earnings engine

The #1 investment bank and the largest markets franchise on Wall Street monetize volatility and deal flow. Q2 proved the operating leverage: CIB revenue $24.85B (+27%), net income $9.68B (+46%) at 22% ROE. Petno’s Q3 preview — IB fees and Markets both up mid-to-high teens y/y — says the engine is still running hot nine days before the print.

3

Asset & Wealth Management compounds quietly

$5.1T in AUM (+18%), $50B of long-term net inflows, total client assets $7.7T (+19%), and a record 44,000 first-time wealth investors in Q2. This is the highest-multiple earnings stream in the bank and its best structural grower — fee revenue without credit risk.

4

Fortress capital funds a double shareholder return

CET1 at 14.1% against ~11–12% requirements leaves ample headroom. The bank pays shareholders twice: a dividend just raised 10% to $1.65 quarterly ($6.60 annualized, ~2.0% forward yield) and a $6.2B quarterly buyback pace that compounds per-share value through the cycle.

5

Scale in technology widens the moat every year

~$18B-plus of annual technology spend — more than most regional banks’ entire revenue — funds the digital experience (63.7M mobile users) and the AI/Agentic tooling now extending into internal workflows. The moat compounds because the spend compounds.

Variant perception. The market is not disputing any of this — the 2.9× book multiple is the market’s verdict that JPMorgan is the best bank. The differentiated question is whether peak-cycle returns can persist long enough to grow into the multiple, or whether the multiple must come down to meet normalized returns. Dimon’s “as good as it gets” is the honest bull case and the honest bear case in one sentence.
03 | Business & Industry

Business quality and competitive position

JPMorgan Chase, roots to 1799, is the largest U.S. bank by assets (~$5.0T) and the most diversified: a consumer bank serving roughly half of American households, the world’s #1 investment bank by fees, the largest markets franchise on Wall Street, and a $5.1T asset-management platform. Over the last five years roughly half of total revenue came from net interest income and half from fee/noninterest sources — the balance that lets the bank earn through both rate regimes.

Competitive position

Against the money-center peers (Bank of America, Citigroup, Wells Fargo) JPMorgan earns structurally higher returns — 23% ROTCE versus mid-teens for the peer group. Against Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley it offers the same Wall Street earnings plus a consumer bank and a fortress balance sheet, at a lower multiple than the pure-play investment banks currently command. The premium to peers is the largest in large-cap banking: deserved, in our view, but it leaves less room for multiple expansion and more downside if returns normalize.

Dimon’s September macro tour. At the bank’s India Investor Conference (Sept. 21–22), Dimon said hyperscaler AI infrastructure spend could exceed $1T next year — adding ~1% to GDP growth on its own — while warning the build-out “may add a little bit to inflation” near term before AI becomes deflationary. He also urged the U.S. not to punish India over Russian-oil purchases (tariffs could push crude past $90) and published a September 29 Wall Street Journal op-ed calling for a U.S.–Europe economic alliance. For the investment case, the read-through is: higher-for-longer rates (NII-positive), persistent geopolitical risk (fee-positive via volatility), and a CEO still firmly at the helm.

06 | Rates

Net interest income & the rate environment

NII is the stock’s fundamental anchor — roughly half of revenue over five years — and the trajectory just improved. Management raised full-year 2026 NII guidance to ~$105.5B (from ~$103B), with NII ex-Markets at ~$96.5B. Q2 NII was $25.51B (+10% y/y), powered by 10% average loan growth ($1.52T) and 7% deposit growth ($2.69T).

The September 16 FOMC hike to 3.75–4.00% — the first increase since July 2023 — is a net positive for the NII arithmetic: higher-for-longer extends the window of elevated net yields, and the bank raised its prime rate to 7% in response. Two yellow flags from the Q2 data deserve attention: the net yield on interest-earning assets slipped to 2.40% (from 2.43%) as funding costs crept up, and noninterest-bearing deposit growth has decelerated to ~6% — the structural deposit advantage is eroding at the margin. Underwrite NII growth from balance-sheet expansion, not margin expansion.

Rate-path risk cuts both ways. Aggressive Fed easing would compress NII even as it helped credit — JPMorgan is asset-sensitive enough for this to matter. But with Brent above $100 on the Iran conflict and Dimon warning inflation is “not clearly defeated,” the near-term skew favors the higher-for-longer path the NII guide assumes.
07 | Credit

Credit quality

Credit remains benign — the single most important confirmation in a late-cycle, higher-rate environment. The Q2 provision fell 12% y/y to $2.52B; net charge-offs were $2.4B; the reserve build was a token $149M. In Card Services, the net charge-off rate improved to 3.34% (from 3.47% in Q1), and management cut the full-year Card NCO guide to ~3.2% (from 3.4%).

The watch items: Card NCO dollars are still trending up ~4% y/y (~$2.0B) — early normalization, not deterioration — and management flagged continued pressure on lower-income households from elevated living costs and rates. For now, the bank is being paid to take credit risk and experiencing almost none of it. Q3’s provision and the Card NCO rate are the two numbers that would first signal a turn.

09 | Management

Management & succession

The June 25, 2026 reshuffle settled the succession question into a clear two-horse race: Doug Petno (CIB chief) and Troy Rohrbaugh (consumer banking chief) were named co-presidents, while longtime heir-apparent Marianne Lake retired. Dimon plans to stay as CEO for about three more years before transitioning to executive chairman, and both contenders received $30M retention RSUs vesting through 2028 tied to a ≥12% ROTCE target. Petno’s confident Q3 preview at the Barclays conference was effectively his first public audition — and he passed it. The residual risk is the one markets always price around successions: whether the next CEO sustains the culture and through-cycle discipline. That is a 2029 problem, not a 2026 one.

11 | Risks

Risks, catalysts & falsifiers

Risks

  • Cyclical peak risk. Record Markets revenue, record IB fees, and “as good as it gets” — the base case must assume normalization, not extrapolation. A capital-markets slowdown hits the highest-beta earnings first.
  • Credit normalization. Card NCO dollars are rising; lower-income consumer stress is acknowledged. A recession would turn the 3.2% Card NCO guide into 5%+ and the provision from tailwind to headwind.
  • Rate-path reversal. Aggressive Fed easing would compress NII even as it helped credit — the bank is asset-sensitive enough for this to matter.
  • Expense creep. Adjusted 2026 expense guide of ~$107.5B is growing faster than core revenue; operating leverage is negative until revenue catches up.
  • Regulatory / capital. Basel endgame calibration and stress-test surprises could raise required capital and slow buybacks.
  • Succession execution. Low probability, high impact — the 2029 transition must preserve the risk culture.
  • Valuation. At 2.9× tangible book, the least margin of safety of any point in the last five years. Quality does not immunize against multiple compression.
