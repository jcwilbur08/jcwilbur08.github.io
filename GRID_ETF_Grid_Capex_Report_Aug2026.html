1. Fund profile, mandate, and cost

GRID (First Trust Nasdaq Clean Edge Smart Grid Infrastructure Index Fund) tracks a modified market-cap-weighted index of global companies engaged in electric grid modernization — grid hardware, transmission & distribution equipment, energy storage and management, and enabling software. Launched November 17, 2009, it has grown into a scale product with $12.17B in AUM as the smart-grid/AI-power theme has re-rated. It is thematic and equity-only, not an income or bond-substitute product, which fits your growth mandate cleanly — the 0.77% trailing yield is not why you'd own it.

Sector and geographic composition

GRID reads as an electrical-equipment and industrial-infrastructure fund with a utility and grid-technology overlay, not a pure regulated-utility income fund. A meaningful share of assets sits outside the US — Schneider Electric (France), ABB (Switzerland), E.ON (Germany, 4.34%), National Grid (UK, 4.18%), Prysmian (Italy, 3.39%), Terna (Italy), Hydro One (Canada), and Korean names HD Hyundai Electric and LS Electric together add up to roughly a third of the fund by weight. This is a different kind of non-US exposure than VXUS — it is thematically concentrated in grid/electrical names rather than broadly diversified, so it should not be counted toward your VXUS build-out target even though it technically adds foreign-domiciled holdings.

3. The capex case: why the grid needs rebuilding now

Utility capital spending is at a record and still rising

Source: S&P Global Market Intelligence, peer group of 44 North American electric utilities, April 2026 report. 2026E/2027E figures are S&P Global's own forward estimates.

Capital spending across the major North American electric-utility peer group has grown from $146B in 2022 to a projected $227.8B in 2026 — a roughly 56% increase in four years with capex for the group forecast to grow to $112.457 billion in 2028 from $108.436 billion in 2026 and $80.547 billion in 2025 for a narrower Financial Focus sub-group, and on the broader 46-company tracked universe, aggregate spending for 2026–2030 is now forecast at a record $1.295 trillion, up from a $1.169 trillion forecast made as recently as December 2025. This is not a speculative multi-year TAM slide — it is capital already appearing in published five-year utility plans, funded through rate base growth and, increasingly, direct hyperscaler co-investment.

Data-center load growth is the dominant driver

Source: composite of BofA Global Research, GridStrategies, and industry estimates cited in Utility Dive and Seeking Alpha coverage (2026). Individual GW estimates vary meaningfully by source and methodology.

Estimates vary by source, but they point the same direction: Bank of America forecasts data centers could add roughly 125 GW of US electric load from 2026 through 2030, pushing overall electricity demand growth to a 4.1% compound annual rate, and BofA projects the US will need more than 230 GW of new generating capacity over the next five years against only about 93 GW of accredited new supply from regulated utilities — a gap exceeding 100 GW. Separately, US data-center power demand is projected to roughly triple from about 60 GW in 2025 to 180 GW by 2030, with 30 GW of giga-scale projects coming online between 2026 and 2027 alone. The bottleneck is now openly regulatory as much as physical: in June 2026 FERC issued a "justify-or-reform" order directing six regional grid operators — PJM, MISO, ERCOT, CAISO, SPP, and NYISO — to either justify existing interconnection timelines or reform their processes to fast-track large loads.

The hyperscaler-funded infrastructure model is becoming standard practice

A structural shift worth noting: hyperscalers are increasingly funding utility infrastructure directly rather than simply waiting in an interconnection queue. Entergy's arrangement with Meta on the Hyperion data-center campus in Louisiana now totals $27 billion, with Meta funding the full infrastructure build-out including roughly 5.2 GW of new gas generation, 240 miles of new transmission, storage, nuclear-plant upgrades, and up to 2.5 GW of renewables. Entergy separately holds agreements with at least three hyperscale data-center developers and has affirmed a target of greater than 8% EPS CAGR alongside 8% retail sales growth through 2029. Among names in your existing book, Southern Company is guiding to a 6% annual load-growth rate driven by data centers, and Duke Energy has outlined a $102.2 billion capital plan — your SO position already carries direct exposure to this exact capex cycle, independent of GRID.

5. Bull case / bear case

CaseArgument
BullGRID is the most diversified, lowest-effort way to own the grid-infrastructure leg of the AI build-out without underwriting single-name execution risk the way GEV requires. The capex tailwind is now visible in five-year utility plans and record backlogs at every top holding, not a forecast. Expense ratio (0.56%) is reasonable for a curated thematic index, and the 16½-year track record with a 12.73% annualized since-inception return suggests the index construction has held up through at least one full cycle (2009–2026, including 2022's bear market).
BearThe fund has already re-rated sharply (+32.5% trailing year, beta 1.27), and several top holdings — Eaton in particular, near 39× trailing earnings against a 24× ten-year average — are pricing continued beat-and-raise as the base case, echoing the same "market pricing the bull case as base" pattern flagged in the GEV report. The top-10 concentration (59.7%) means the "129 holdings" diversification claim is partly cosmetic. A third of assets sit in foreign-currency names (EUR, GBP, CHF, KRW) whose FX and regulatory exposure is a different risk than the low-cost, broadly-diversified international exposure VXUS is meant to provide. And because GRID's top names move on the same order-book sentiment as GEV, AVGO, and the hyperscalers, a cooling in AI-capex enthusiasm would likely hit this fund and your existing concentrated names simultaneously — not offset each other.

6. What to monitor next

  1. Q3 2026 prints from Eaton, Quanta, ABB, and Schneider (roughly Oct–Nov 2026). The thing to watch is whether data-center order growth is still accelerating or has started to normalize off 2026's triple-digit comparisons — the first material deceleration is the early tell for the whole theme, same as the GEV order-growth watch item.
  2. Backlog-to-revenue conversion timing. Quanta's own management flagged that its largest transmission projects don't enter backlog until H2'26 and won't see field activity until H2'27 — a multi-year lag between headline order growth and recognized revenue that applies across most of GRID's top holdings given transformer/equipment lead times running 3–5 years.
  3. FERC's large-load interconnection reforms. The June 2026 order is a genuine structural tailwind if RTOs actually accelerate queues; slippage in implementation would be a real negative for the whole grid-buildout thesis, GRID included.
  4. Valuation vs. your GEV thesis. If you decide to add to GEV on the weakness flagged in the July report, revisit whether a GRID add makes more sense at that same moment — the two theses move together.
