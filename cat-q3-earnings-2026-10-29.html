Section 01 · Desk decision

The verdict and the entry point

Buy is maintained, conviction stays at 6 out of 10, and the $850 target is unchanged. What changed on Thursday is the price — and the price did exactly what the pre-earnings report said it needed to do. The entry point is not a prediction that was vindicated in the abstract. It was a level, and the stock traded through it.

Thursday's print was good enough to defend the thesis and not good enough to strengthen it. The backlog reached a record, Power & Energy beat on both sales and profit, and guidance was maintained. At the same time the earnings beat narrowed from roughly 31% in the second quarter to about 2%, the sales line missed, the margin outlook sits near the bottom of its target range once tariff recoveries are excluded, and the fourth-quarter guide is described only as “moderate.” That combination — a durable demand story, a thinner beat, and no raise — is why conviction does not move above 6. A stronger print would have earned a higher number. This one did not.

Entry-point verdict

The ≤$670 preferred entry is validated and held. Do not move it up. Do not remove it. The staged second tranche of $640–$650 was reached.

Thursday's close of $647.39 sits $22.61 below the $670 entry and inside the $640–$650 second-tranche zone. On the staging set out before the print, the first tranche at or below $670 would already have filled on the way down, and the second tranche is now actionable at the close. The close is not a new target to chase lower, and the entry is not to be re-anchored to Thursday's low. Hold $670 as the reference entry; treat $640–$650 as the completed staging zone.

The invalidation triggers did not fire. The backlog rose to a record $78.1 billion, above the $72.1 billion trigger, and guidance was maintained rather than cut — so the joint trigger failed on both legs. The $606 price floor was not breached: the close stands $41.39 above it, and even the intraday low of $631.01 held $25.01 above it. No gate is installed by this report; the entry point governs a new position only, and Caterpillar is not held.

Why hold, rather than raise or remove, the entry

The evidence against moving the entry up is the quality of the beat. A 2% earnings beat built partly on $128 million of tariff recoveries, a revenue miss, a fourth quarter framed as “moderate,” a margin outlook near the bottom of its range excluding recoveries, and a roughly $1 billion year-on-year dealer-inventory headwind in the fourth quarter all argue that the de-rating from about 25 times forward earnings at Wednesday's close to about 23 times at Thursday's close may not be finished if January's 2027 outlook disappoints.

The evidence against removing the entry is the demand position. A record backlog, 58% of it expected to be sold within twelve months, orders stretching into 2029 and 2030, a Power & Energy business constrained by capacity rather than demand, $3.8 billion of quarterly machinery free cash flow, and guidance maintained rather than cut. The print validated the entry level. It weakened the case for chasing above $670, and it did not weaken the case for the level itself.

Levels to watch from here: $606 (invalidation floor), $631.01 (Thursday's low), $670 (entry reference). The next fundamental tests are fourth-quarter delivery and the 2027 outlook in January.

Section 02 · Correction, stated plainly

Read the consensus right

Before the print, coverage quoted a consensus range for the third quarter. That range is superseded. This report uses one locked source throughout, and the verdicts below follow from that source alone.

Correction: the pre-earnings consensus range is superseded

The pre-earnings coverage quoted a third-quarter consensus range of $6.92–$7.12 on earnings per share and $20.4–$20.8 billion on revenue, drawn from mixed providers. That range is superseded by the locked single-source figure used throughout this report: FactSet — adjusted earnings per share $7.06, revenue $20.76 billion.

Against FactSet, revenue of $20,682 million missed by $78 million (−0.4%) and adjusted earnings per share of $7.20 beat by $0.14 (+2.0%). FactSet sat inside the old range — the range was unusable because it mixed providers, not because it was far off. Do not use the Zacks set ($7.12 / $20.58 billion), which would falsely read the quarter as a revenue beat.

Section 03 · The quarter

The print: a thin beat

Revenue rose 17.3% to $20,682 million — the highest sales quarter in the company's history — and adjusted profit per share rose 45.5% to $7.20. The market's objection was not to the growth. It was to the shape of it: a beat that narrowed sharply, on a sales line that missed, with no raise attached.

In the second quarter, adjusted earnings of $8.17 beat expectations by roughly 31%. In the third quarter the beat was about 2%. That compression is the single most important change in the quarter's read-across. A company that had beaten and raised in both the first and second quarters of 2026 beat narrowly and held in the third. The streak, in the street's shorthand, ended.

What drove the growth

Volume did the work. The sales bridge in the release shows volume adding $2,207 million, price adding $663 million, currency adding $58 million, and Financial Products adding $116 million. Price rose in all three primary segments. Manufacturing costs were unfavourable by $415 million on a consolidated basis, including freight and the costs of expanding capacity — the other side of the power-demand story, and a reminder that rapid growth is not free to deliver.

The effective tax rate was 22.9% in the quarter against 26.7% a year earlier; excluding discrete items, the estimated annual rate is 23.0% against 24.0%. Buybacks added roughly $0.15 to adjusted earnings per share year on year. None of these items changes the headline read, but together they explain why a 45.5% rise in adjusted earnings per share should not be read as a 45.5% rise in underlying earning power.

The tape on Thursday

The stock fell before the open and never recovered the $670 level during the session. Premarket trading took it 9.7% lower to $644.03. The regular session opened at $636.33, traded between $631.01 and $664.47, and closed at $647.39, down $65.99 or 9.25% from Wednesday's $713.38 close. The intraday high of $664.47 never reached $670. The session, in other words, delivered the entry structure in full: through the first level, into the second-tranche zone, and holding above the floor.

Section 04 · Segment detail

Segments: power carried it

Power & Energy beat FactSet on sales by $564 million and grew profit 31%. Construction Industries missed on sales but beat comfortably on profit. Resource Industries grew 15% with a margin that slipped a tenth of a point. The quarter's centre of gravity is unmistakable: it sits in power generation.

Power & Energy: the engine, and the constraint

Power-generation sales within the segment reached $3,421 million, up 30% year on year from $2,634 million. Sales to users in power generation rose 75%, according to the call summary. The segment's margin widened 2.5 points to 24.4% on volume and price, with price contributing $216 million. This is the business behind the backlog, the capacity programme, and the multi-year demand visibility discussed on the call — and it is also the business where bringing additional capacity online remains, in the chief executive's words, the top pacing factor for growth.

Construction Industries: profit strength, sales softness

Construction sales of $7,983 million missed FactSet by $137 million, but profit of $1,831 million rose 33% and margin widened 2.5 points to 22.9%. Price contributed $349 million, the largest price contribution of any segment. The sales miss alongside the profit beat points to mix and pricing doing more work than volume in the quarter — and to the dealer-inventory dynamics that become a headwind in the fourth quarter, covered in Section 07.

Resource Industries: steady, with ramp friction

Resource sales rose 15% to $4,615 million and profit rose 14% to $622 million, but margin slipped 0.1 point to 13.5%. Manufacturing costs were unfavourable by $208 million in the segment, including material costs and tariffs. On the call, the chief executive described three strong quarters of orders in Resource Industries alongside operational challenges and inefficiencies as production ramps. The segment is growing; it is not yet growing cleanly.

Backlog: the record, in context

Backlog from the call and slides, not the release. The third-quarter 2025 figure is approximate, derived as $78.1 billion less the $38.2 billion year-on-year increase. Backlog grew $6.0 billion sequentially, after a $9.0 billion sequential increase in the second quarter — the level rose, the pace of growth cooled, and the share expected to be sold within twelve months slipped from 59% to 58%.

Section 05 · Earnings call

The call: capacity, not demand

Chief executive Joe Creed and chief financial officer Kyle Epley described a company whose problem is producing fast enough, not selling fast enough — and a company that will not yet put numbers on 2027. Both halves of that message matter for the entry decision.

“Our third quarter results reflect the durability of our end markets with our highest sales quarter ever, strong order rates, and a record backlog. Our team continues to execute our strategic priorities in a dynamic environment, positioning us for a strong year.” — Joe Creed, chief executive, opening remarks

Creed put the backlog in plain terms: sales and revenues of $20.7 billion, up 17%, adjusted profit per share of $7.20, up 45%, and a record $78 billion backlog, up approximately $38 billion or 96% from a year earlier — the fourth consecutive quarter of sequential backlog growth in all three primary segments. Fifty-eight percent of the backlog is expected to be sold within the next twelve months, against 59% last quarter, implying roughly $45.3 billion deliverable inside a year.

Demand visibility runs to 2029 and 2030

On lead times, Creed was specific. Large reciprocating-engine and turbine orders extend into late 2028 and into 2029, with some orders in 2029 and 2030, and smaller generator sets and engines on much shorter lead times. The company has, in his words, a line of sight on demand past the backlog, stretching up to 2030 and beyond that in some cases. Frame agreements add visibility beyond the booked backlog itself, and prime-power projects — negotiated individually and lumpy by nature — can arrive in the backlog in large pieces. Backlog growth in the quarter was broad-based across oil and gas, power generation, Resource Industries, and Construction Industries.

“Specifically, our Power & Energy team continued to increase production of large reciprocating engines and turbines while simultaneously expanding capacity to meet demand. The team is effectively managing the operational challenges that come with this rapid growth. Bringing additional capacity online is still the top pacing factor for growth in Power & Energy.” — Joe Creed, chief executive

The capacity programme behind that statement was framed before the print: a plan to nearly triple large-engine capacity against 2024 and more than triple power-generation sales by 2030. Creed's preference, stated on the call, is to bring the production ramp closer to sales to users. That is a multi-year programme, not a quarterly fix, and it is the reason the margin recovery discussed below is framed over “the next couple of years” rather than the next quarter.

On margins: a climb from the bottom of the range

“I would expect … that we start to claw our way off the bottom of that margin target range as we go forward … over the next couple of years, we want to try to work our way back to the midpoint.” — Joe Creed, chief executive, on the adjusted operating margin framework

That framing should be read alongside the guidance in Section 06: the full-year adjusted operating margin, excluding tariff recoveries, sits near the bottom of the target range. The direction Creed describes is upward, over years, from a low starting point. It is not a promise for the fourth quarter, and it should not be underwritten as one.

What the call did not do

The call deferred 2027. Creed's answer on the year ahead was that the company would talk more about 2027 in January, with a strong backlog, longer line of sight, and strong sales to users expected across all three primary segments into 2027. No numeric 2027 guidance was given, and none should be inferred from the backlog arithmetic alone. January is now the next fundamental test of this thesis, alongside fourth-quarter delivery.

Section 06 · Outlook

Power & Energy is expected to grow moderately in the fourth quarter, with a margin similar to the third quarter and higher year on year. Section 07 · Costs and channels

Tariffs and dealer inventory

Two mechanics will shape the next two quarters more than any single demand signal: tariff costs with shrinking recoveries, and a large seasonal dealer-inventory reduction in the fourth quarter. Both were quantified on the call. Both cut against the fourth quarter.

Tariffs: $600 million in the quarter, recoveries fading

Chief financial officer Kyle Epley put third-quarter tariff costs at approximately $600 million, excluding tariff recoveries, about flat year on year, as anticipated. The company recognised $128 million in tariff recoveries in the quarter, nearly all of it in corporate items, against $392 million in the second quarter and approximately $520 million year to date. The full-year tariff cost remains approximately $2.2 billion excluding recoveries, with approximately $600 million expected in the fourth quarter — similar to the third quarter but roughly $200 million lower than the fourth quarter of 2025 — and no additional recoveries assumed in the outlook.

The direction matters more than any single quarter. Recoveries fell from $392 million to $128 million between the second and third quarters, and the fourth-quarter outlook assumes none. The tariff drag is therefore becoming cleaner — and more fully borne in the margin — even as the gross cost stays flat. That is one reason the margin outlook sits near the bottom of its range excluding recoveries, and why the recoveries should not be annualised when modelling the fourth quarter.

Dealer inventory: a modest headwind now, a $1 billion one next

Construction Industries dealer inventory rose $300 million in the third quarter, against a $600 million rise in the third quarter of 2025 — a modest headwind to sales volume, in line with expectations. The fourth quarter reverses the pattern at larger scale: an anticipated seasonal reduction of approximately $1.6 billion, against a $600 million decline a year earlier, creating an approximately $1 billion year-on-year volume headwind. Year-end Construction Industries dealer inventory is still expected to finish approximately $700 million above the end of 2025, in anticipation of strong sales to users in 2027. The destock is seasonal and deliberate, not a demand signal — but it is a real volume headwind all the same, and it is already in the “moderate” fourth-quarter framing.

Section 08 · Cash generation

Cash flow and returns

The quarter generated cash at a scale that supports the backlog thesis: $3.8 billion of machinery free cash flow, $4.4 billion of enterprise operating cash flow, and $2.0 billion returned to shareholders — while the balance sheet ended the quarter with $7.9 billion of enterprise cash.

Cash in the quarter

Machinery, Power & Energy free cash flow was $3.8 billion in the third quarter, up approximately $0.6 billion year on year, on capital expenditure of approximately $0.7 billion in the quarter. Enterprise operating cash flow was $4.4 billion. Enterprise cash stood at $7.9 billion at September 30. The full-year machinery free-cash-flow target range of $6–15 billion, with the outcome expected in its top half, was maintained.

Returns in the quarter

The company deployed $2.0 billion to shareholders: $1.2 billion of share repurchases and approximately $0.7 billion of dividends. The dividend of $1.63 per quarter — $6.52 annualised, a yield of approximately 1.0% at Thursday's $647.39 close — was maintained by the board on October 14 and is payable November 19, with a record date of October 26. There was no increase at this declaration.

One credit detail from the call is worth recording. Cat Financial past dues stood at 1.31%, described as the lowest level recorded since 1998, with retail new business volume up 6%. The allowance for credit losses was 0.91% ($332 million), against 0.84% at June 30. Nothing in the financing arm contradicts the demand story; the slight rise in the allowance bears watching, but at this level it is a footnote, not a flag.

Cash is also the cleanest answer to the question of whether the power programme is being funded or merely described. A business generating $3.8 billion of quarterly machinery free cash flow while expanding capacity, returning $2.0 billion, and holding $7.9 billion of cash is funding the programme from operations. The open question is not funding. It is execution — whether capacity arrives fast enough, and at what cost, as Resource Industries' ramp friction already shows.

Section 09 · Price, multiple, and the street

Valuation and the street

Thursday's fall compressed a premium multiple. It did not eliminate it. At $647.39 the stock still trades above its five-year average forward multiple, still sits well below the street's pre-print targets, and still carries a $850 desk target that now requires 31.3% — a demanding, but no longer implausible, move from a lower base.

Where the price sits: Thursday's close against the levels that matter

Scale is linear from $600. The street mean of approximately $947 is the pre-print consensus target; no post-print consensus mean was verified. The 52-week high of $1,073.46 was set June 30, 2026; Thursday's close sits 39.7% below it.

The multiple: compressed, not cheap

No single settled forward price-to-earnings figure exists for this stock — provider and vintage conflicts prevent one, and this report does not average them into a false precision. On simple arithmetic, $647.39 divided by $27.74 of consensus earnings per share for 2026 gives approximately 23.3 times. That sits against a five-year average forward multiple of approximately 19.3–19.9 times: roughly 17–21% above history on that arithmetic. Other readings frame the same move differently — approximately 25 times at Wednesday's close, and approximately 22 times in premarket trading — but every reading agrees on the direction: the premium compressed on Thursday, and it was not eliminated. The 2027 consensus figures in circulation, at approximately $32.42–$32.44 of earnings per share, are pre-print expectations; January's outlook, not the backlog alone, will decide whether they hold.

The desk's $850 target sits below the pre-print street mean of approximately $947 and above the two most cautious pre-print targets, at $749–$750. In other words, the desk target already embedded the October de-rating before Thursday's fall moved the stock further below all of them. At Thursday's close, the target implies 31.3% upside; the street mean implies approximately 46%; the most cautious targets imply approximately 16%. The spread between those numbers is a measure of how little agreement remains about what this company is worth at 23 times earnings with no 2027 numbers yet.

Section 10 · Falsification and watchpoints

What would change the call

A Buy at 6 out of 10 with a held entry point is a conditional call. These are the conditions — stated before the next print, so that the next print can be judged against them rather than fitted to them.

The invalidation triggers, restated

The joint trigger from the pre-earnings report still governs: a backlog below $72.1 billion and a guidance cut, together, would invalidate the entry thesis. Neither leg occurred on Thursday. The price trigger is separate: a close below $606 forces a fresh review regardless of the backlog. Thursday's low of $631.01 came $25.01 above that floor; it did not test it.

What to watch, in order

  • Fourth-quarter delivery. “Moderate” growth, a $1.6 billion Construction Industries dealer-inventory reduction, a margin stepping down sequentially in Construction, and $600 million of tariff costs with no recoveries assumed. A fourth quarter that lands below “moderate” — or a margin that slips further from the bottom of the range — would put the January outlook under real pressure.
  • January's 2027 outlook. The call deferred every 2027 number to January. The backlog supports a strong 2027; the multiple at 23 times requires one. If January's outlook disappoints, the de-rating that began in October has further to run, and the $670 entry — validated on price — would need to be re-examined on fundamentals.
  • Backlog momentum, not just the level. Sequential growth cooled from $9.0 billion in the second quarter to $6.0 billion in the third, and the sub-twelve-month share slipped from 59% to 58%. The level trigger did not fire. A further deceleration, or a quarter in which the backlog falls outright, would change the read even before the $72.1 billion level is reached.
  • Capacity execution and cost. Power & Energy is capacity-constrained, Resource Industries is already showing ramp inefficiencies, and consolidated manufacturing costs were $415 million unfavourable in the quarter. The programme to nearly triple large-engine capacity is the thesis; its cost and pace are the risk.
  • Tariff recoveries ending. Recoveries fell from $392 million to $128 million and are assumed at zero in the fourth quarter. Any further tariff escalation without offsetting recovery or pricing would fall directly on the margin Creed expects to claw back over years.
  • The street's post-print revisions. Only one reiteration had appeared by Thursday evening. The October 30 notes may cut targets further. A wave of post-print cuts would not by itself change the desk call, but it would signal that the market's 9.25% verdict was the consensus view, not an overreaction.

What could not be verified

Stated plainly, so that nothing in this report rests on it: the October 26 close of $692.13; a Resource Industries consensus figure from FactSet; the numeric adjusted operating-margin target range; any numeric 2027 guidance; any October 29 post-print target cut beyond the Oppenheimer reiteration; and the 52-week low, for which conflicting figures circulate. No after-hours close was verified. Where a figure could not be verified, this report says so or omits it — it does not estimate it.

Bottom line. The quarter defended the thesis and disciplined the price. The entry point held, the second tranche was reached, the triggers did not fire, and conviction did not rise. Hold the $670 entry, respect the $606 floor, and let the fourth quarter and January do the next piece of work.
