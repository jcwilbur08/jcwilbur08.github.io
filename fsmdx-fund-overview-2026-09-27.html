1. Executive Summary & Verdict

FSMDX is the mid-cap sibling of FSSNX and it is, if anything, the better of the two: a 4-star fund tracking the Russell Midcap Index at 0.025%, with roughly $53 billion in assets and tracking error running at ~0.05%. In the first quarter of 2026 it returned 1.30% against the index's 1.29% — that is what a well-run index fund looks like.

The portfolio question here is sharper than the FSSNX case, because Cam's other mid-cap vehicle — IJH, the iShares Core S&P Mid-Cap ETF (~$19.8k, 0.05%) — tracks a different index (S&P MidCap 400). That makes the duplication less obvious but no less real: two funds, ~$50k combined (~3% of the portfolio), both delivering US mid-cap beta with heavily overlapping sector exposures. The honest answer in section 7: the book needs one mid-cap core, and FSMDX is the survivor — cheaper (half the fee), broader (800 vs 400 stocks), and better-rated (4 stars vs 3).

Bottom line: KEEP FSMDX as the mid-cap core in its account. The IJH consolidation originally recommended here is unavailable — FSMDX and IJH sit in different retirement accounts — so each account keeps its own mid-cap vehicle. There is no case for selling mid-cap exposure itself: it fills the gap between the large-cap growth book and the small-cap sleeves, and the 1.80% weight is modest.

2. Objective & Index Tracked

FSMDX seeks results that correspond to the total return of US mid-cap stocks, investing at least 80% of assets in securities of the Russell Midcap Index — the bottom 800 stocks of the Russell 1000, i.e., companies ranked 201 through 1,000 by market capitalization. It is market-cap weighted, reconstituted annually by FTSE Russell, and crucially has no profitability screen: a mid-cap company is included on size alone, profitable or not.

The Russell Midcap is the default institutional mid-cap benchmark — broader than the S&P 400 and constructed purely on size. Its top end reaches up to what many would call large caps and its bottom end bleeds into small-cap territory, which is why it behaves as a smoother bridge between the two segments.

The indexes genuinely differ. The Russell Midcap is the bottom 800 of the Russell 1000 — pure market-cap ranking, no quality filter, so it holds unprofitable mid caps alongside solid earners, reaches slightly higher into large-cap territory at the top, and dips deeper into small-cap territory at the bottom. The S&P MidCap 400 is a committee-selected 400 with an earnings requirement: constituents must be profitable, which tilts it toward established earners and gives it a modest quality bias. The sector and style differences are real but second-order — both funds end up dominated by industrials, financials, tech, and healthcare names in similar proportions.

But "different index" is not the same as "diversification." The honest test is whether holding both changes Cam's risk profile in any meaningful way. It does not:

  • Correlation is near-perfect. Russell Midcap and S&P MidCap 400 move together — both are US mid-cap beta with heavy overlap in membership and sector weights. The quality screen changes the flavor, not the risk exposure.
  • Neither position is big enough to be a bet. At 1.80% and 1.16%, neither vehicle is sized as a conviction position; they are both "mid-cap exposure," and the split buys nothing.
  • The fee goes the wrong way. The smaller, duplicate position (IJH) costs twice as much. Paying 0.05% on $19.8k for the same mid-cap beta available at 0.025% is a pure drag with no offsetting benefit.
  • Simplicity has value. Two funds, two statements lines, two distribution schedules, two tax lots — for what is effectively one exposure.
Recommended mid-cap structure (advisory): KEEP FSMDX as the mid-cap core in its account at 0.025% — broad Russell Midcap exposure (805 stocks), 4-star rated. IJH stays as the mid-cap vehicle in its own account; the two cannot be merged across retirement accounts. The overlap is a venial cross-account redundancy, not an actionable one — the case would reopen only if the accounts are ever combined.

Sizing judgment: the consolidated ~3% mid-cap weight sits comfortably between the large-cap-dominated core and the ~4.5% small-cap sleeve — a textbook size-segment ladder for a retirement portfolio. No trim or add is needed on the segment; the fix is structural, not sizing.

8. Risks — What Could Change the Verdict

  • Mid-cap cycle risk. As with FSSNX, the risk is the segment, not the fund: mid caps are economically sensitive and underperform in narrow, mega-cap-led rallies. The 10-year shortfall vs the S&P 500 is the visible cost of that cyclicality.
  • Rate sensitivity. Mid caps carry more leverage and floating-rate exposure than large caps; the September hike and signaled further tightening are a headwind for the index's earnings. Watch the October FOMC.
  • Tracking breakdown. Would matter only if tracking error widened materially — it runs at ~0.05%, no concern.
  • Fee increase or benchmark change. Same as FSSNX: not a real risk — Fidelity uses these funds as franchise builders.
  • Index methodology shifts. FTSE Russell periodically adjusts reconstitution rules; a material change in the Midcap band definition would alter what the fund owns. No such change is on the table.

Appendix B — Glossary

NAV — net asset value: a mutual fund's per-share price, set once daily after market close. Expense ratio — annual operating cost as a percentage of assets; 0.025% = $2.50 per $10,000 per year. Tracking error — how closely a fund follows its index; smaller is better. Russell Midcap — the bottom 800 stocks of the Russell 1000 (ranks 201–1,000 by market cap); the default institutional mid-cap benchmark. S&P MidCap 400 — 400 committee-selected US mid-cap companies with a profitability requirement. Securities lending — the fund lends portfolio stocks to short-sellers for a fee, offsetting expenses. Reconstitution — the index's annual refresh of membership, which index funds must trade through.
