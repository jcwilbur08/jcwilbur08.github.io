Healthcare: durable demand, divergent economics

The sector's spending base is enormous and still growing faster than GDP, but the investable opportunity is not “buy healthcare.” The best setups combine recurring revenue, regulatory or workflow moats, and a valuation that does not already capitalize the full growth story.

Executive view

Investment conclusion

Healthcare offers above-economy demand growth, but the profit pool is bifurcating. Branded pharmaceuticals, differentiated medtech, life-science tools, specialty pharmacy and data-enabled services retain the strongest economics. Labor-heavy providers and government-exposed managed care face the sharpest policy and cost pressure. My highest-priority research candidates are Bristol Myers Squibb, Intuitive Surgical, Abbott, Thermo Fisher and Elevance Health.

Portfolio implication: for a retirement-oriented portfolio, favor a basket rather than one concentrated bet: one quality medtech compounder, one life-science-tools platform, and one value/recovery name. Treat the managed-care idea as the smallest and most evidence-dependent position.

Industry overview

Market size and five-year dynamics

There is no single non-duplicative “healthcare revenue” number because payments flow through insurers, providers, pharmacies and manufacturers. The cleanest auditable market-size anchor is expenditure. CMS reports U.S. health spending of $5.3 trillion in 2024, equal to 18.0% of GDP, and projects 5.4% average annual growth during 2025–34 to $9.0 trillion. The next-five-year run rate is reasonably framed at about 5%–5.5% nominal CAGR, with faster near-term utilization and drug growth followed by policy-driven moderation. [CMS NHE fact sheet]

Globally, WHO's latest aggregate release puts 2023 health spending at $10.6 trillion, approximately 10% of world GDP. This is a spending measure—not an investable revenue pool—and cross-country reimbursement and affordability vary widely. [WHO global expenditure update]

Structural tailwinds

  • Aging: the U.S. 65+ population reached 61.2 million in 2024, and the oldest cohorts are entering higher-acuity years. [U.S. Census]
  • Chronic disease and obesity: sustained demand for diabetes, cardiovascular, oncology and musculoskeletal care.
  • Innovation: GLP-1s, oncology combinations, cell therapy, robotic surgery, liquid biopsy and AI-enabled diagnostics expand addressable markets.
  • Utilization normalization: procedure volumes remain supportive for medtech and diagnostics.
  • Medicare mix: CMS expects Medicare spending to grow 7.7% annually over 2025–34, faster than private insurance or Medicaid. [CMS projection summary]

Structural headwinds

  • Affordability and policy: public and employer payers are intensifying pressure on drug prices, site of care and utilization.
  • Labor: clinicians remain scarce and expensive; providers have limited pricing flexibility.
  • Government-program margin pressure: Medicaid acuity, Medicare Advantage coding/payment changes and coverage churn raise earnings volatility.
  • Tariffs and China: imported inputs, local procurement and pricing pressure weigh on tools and medtech. S&P flags tariffs and insurer stress among key 2026 issues. [S&P Global]
  • Patent cliffs: hundreds of billions of branded-drug revenue face loss of exclusivity this decade.
Value chain and profit pools

Where the economics are strongest

Manufacturers capture the largest absolute profit pool, but margin and capital efficiency differ substantially. NYU Stern's January 2026 U.S. sector data show pharmaceuticals with a 26.4% after-tax operating margin and 29.3% return on capital, healthcare products at 15.1% and 22.3%, and healthcare IT at 13.9% and 17.3%. Support services can earn high returns on capital despite thin margins because asset turnover is exceptionally high. [Damodaran sector data]

Competitive moats

Why incumbents remain difficult to displace

Regulatory and evidence barriers

FDA approvals, manufacturing validation, post-market surveillance and reimbursement evidence create years of lead time and large sunk costs. In pharma, a patent is only the beginning: formulary placement, physician confidence and real-world evidence matter. In devices, the product must also be embedded in clinical practice.

Workflow and switching costs

Laboratories validate instruments and assays; hospitals train surgeons and nurses; payers integrate claims, provider networks and pharmacy data. A competing product must be meaningfully better—not merely cheaper—to justify retraining, revalidation and operational risk.

Installed-base economics

Robotic systems, diagnostic platforms and laboratory instruments create recurring instruments, reagents, service and software revenue. This razor-and-blade model improves visibility and makes incumbency self-reinforcing.

Scale, data and distribution

Large payers and distributors negotiate nationally, spread compliance costs, manage working capital and aggregate data across millions of lives. Large manufacturers can fund multiple clinical programs and global launches simultaneously.
