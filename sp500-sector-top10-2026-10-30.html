Information Technology: buy the equipment sell-off, hold the platforms

NEUTRAL — for new money (opinion)

The structural driver is unchanged: AI training and inference demand is pulling through the whole stack — accelerators, high-bandwidth memory, advanced packaging, networking, power and the software that orchestrates it. Third-quarter numbers proved the point: Technology led revenue growth of all eleven sectors, semiconductor earnings were expected up about 130% year on year, and the sector net margin hit 32.3% against a 5-year average of 25.9%. US third-quarter GDP grew 3.4% annualised, with information-processing equipment investment up 37.4%. That is a genuine capex supercycle, not just multiple expansion — forward earnings for the index are up 31% year to date while price is up 11.4%.

The cycle position turned in October. The semiconductor index had fallen 26% from its June peak by late October; the semiconductor index dropped 7.2% on October 30 alone; Intel's guidance warning (−28.58% to $65.76) and Amazon's AI-spending disclosure reframed the question from “can they build it?” to “who pays, at what return, and how is it financed?” — with the 10-year near 5% and leveraged AI financing in focus. Enterprise software was already down about 7% in the week to October 19. This reads as a financing-cost de-rating, not yet a demand collapse: Micron said more than 75% of fiscal-2027 shipments are committed and expects tighter memory markets in 2027–28.

Key risks are customer concentration and circular AI financing (chipmaker ↔ cloud ↔ model-lab stakes); China and memory headlines and export controls; margin normalisation — a 32% sector margin is a record and the single largest assumption inside the index forward multiple; and power and permitting bottlenecks that favour equipment and memory over undifferentiated software.

Valuation: Technology forward P/E is 19.0× now versus 26.5× at end-2025, against an index forward P/E of 18.7× (below its 5-year 19.8× and 10-year 19.1× averages). The sector's own forward P/E versus its 10-year average is not verified — no figure is quoted. At 19.0×, Technology is no longer obviously expensive if earnings hold — but the multiple has compressed precisely because the market doubts they will. That argues for quality and balance-sheet strength over story stocks. Dated context only (trailing, not forward, October 24 snapshot): trailing P/E 27.5×, estimated profit growth 26%.

Concentration and your book: the sector fund's top three were ~39% of the fund; NVIDIA alone was 15.5% of it on September 30. You already hold NVIDIA, Microsoft, Apple, Broadcom, Qualcomm and — outside the index, flagged below — TSMC and ASML, plus Adobe, Salesforce and Dell just outside this Top 10 on valuation and overlap. Opinion: cap any single Technology name at ~5% for new money and fund adds by trimming, not by adding cash, unless a buy level below is hit. Your direct Technology weight is 17.5% versus ~39.9% for the index — the largest underweight in your book — but your funds already fill much of that gap at market weight.

Health Care: rotation, GLP-1 economics, and prices that finally work

OVERWEIGHT — for new money (opinion)

Health Care led the six-week rotation into late October as money left AI-linked technology for defensives after sticky inflation data. The structural drivers are intact and, for once, aligned: GLP-1 obesity and diabetes demand is a multi-decade volume story; medtech — robotics, structural heart, diabetes technology — is in a product-cycle upswing; life-science tools recovered sharply on earnings and upgrades; and managed care is stabilising after a brutal 2025 cost-trend shock. UnitedHealth is already held and was part of the defensive bid.

The cycle position is mid-recovery, not peak. All sectors grew third-quarter earnings, but Health Care margins were below their 5-year average — the only major laggard on quality of earnings — and expected third-quarter EPS growth was just +1.2% in mid-October attributed estimates. That is the opportunity and the risk in one number: the sector is cheap relative to its history because pricing politics, Medicare Advantage cost trends and patent cliffs are real. Reported drug-pricing deals cut realised prices — Lilly's realised prices fell 15% in the third quarter — but volume, up 53%, overwhelmed price, and tariff reprieves plus US manufacturing commitments de-risked the near term.

Key risks: pricing policy spreading beyond GLP-1s to primary-care and oncology drugs; GLP-1 competition — oral products on both sides, with Lilly's oral at $184m in the third quarter versus $217m expected in attributed reporting — and adherence and payer pushback; Medicare Advantage medical-cost trends for the insurers; and the Medicare negotiation overhang for legacy pharma.

Valuation: Lilly at 30.89× forward is the exception — most of the sector trades on mid-teens forward earnings, though per-name multiples are largely not confirmed in this run and must be verified before sizing. The sector's forward P/E versus its 10-year average is not verified. Dated context only (trailing, October 24): trailing P/E 22.8×, estimated profit growth 5%. Multiples expanded in late October even as the sector rotated up — pay for growth only where it is proven. Opinion: that dispersion is the trade — pay up only for Lilly, Boston Scientific and Intuitive growth; buy the rest for yield and mean reversion.

Concentration: the sector fund's top three (Lilly, Johnson & Johnson, AbbVie) were ~33% of the fund; Lilly alone was 15.2% on October 1. Size Lilly as a growth position, not a defensive one. For your book this is the cleanest decision in the report: 2.6% direct versus ~9.1% index, with the stance agreeing — buy priority #1.
