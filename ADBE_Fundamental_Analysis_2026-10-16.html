The one-paragraph version. Adobe is a 35%-GAAP-margin, 40%-FCF-margin subscription franchise still growing roughly 10% organically, and the market is pricing it as if that is about to end: the stock is 33% below its October 2025 high and trades at 13.5x FY26 GAAP earnings (10.0x on the company’s non-GAAP figure). Three adjustments change how good that looks. First, the cheap-looking 10x is a ~13.1x stock once stock comp (8% of revenue) is counted as the cost it is. Second, about 2 points of Q3’s 12% constant-currency growth is the Semrush acquisition, so organic growth is ~10% and the Q4 guide implies ~8%. Third, roughly 3 points of the 13.6% EPS CAGR since FY21 is simply a shrinking share count, and the FY26 buyback has been executed at an average of ~$265, above today’s price. The crux is whether generative AI expands Adobe’s creative and document franchise or commoditizes it, and Adobe stopped reporting the metric (Digital Media ARR) that would answer it. My base case (7.5% revenue CAGR, 35.5% GAAP margin, 13x exit, 3% annual share shrink) returns +11.0% a year; weighted 30/50/20 across bear, base and bull it is 9.9%, essentially the 10% hurdle. At $244.50 you are being paid fairly, not generously, for an unresolved disruption question and a CEO/CFO transition. My view: a hold at the 0.50% starter size, not an exit and not an add.

1. History, business model, and revenue mix

The short history

Shantanu Narayen has run Adobe for eighteen years and led its conversion from boxed software to the Creative Cloud subscription model, the move that created today’s recurring-revenue base. The last strategic chapter was messy. The $20B Figma acquisition, announced in 2022, was abandoned in December 2023 under regulatory pressure, and Adobe paid a $1.0B termination fee that landed in FY24 results. Adobe then pivoted to generative AI (Firefly) and to tuck-in M&A: Semrush (closed April 28, 2026, $1.87B in cash) and Topaz Labs (agreed, expected to close in Q4).

The leadership picture changed substantially in six months. Narayen announced in March 2026 that he would step down once a successor was named; CFO Dan Durn left in June (SVP Steve Day is interim); and Anil Chakravarthy, who runs the Customer Experience unit, becomes CEO on December 1 with Narayen as executive chair. The press has noted that his unit is smaller than the flagship creative business, which is the point to remember when the AI-disruption debate turns to Creative Cloud.

Business model

Adobe sells subscriptions to creative professionals, knowledge workers and marketers, and about 97% of revenue is subscription. It ends Q3 with $27.5B of total ARR (+11.2%) and $22.2B of remaining performance obligations, about two-thirds of which is expected to convert within twelve months. The economics are exceptional because the product is software with almost no physical footprint: capex was 0.8% of revenue in FY25 and the company converts about 40 cents of each revenue dollar into free cash flow. There is no dividend. Cash is returned through buybacks, which is the central capital-allocation fact in this report.

2. Moat and competitive landscape

Where the moat is real

  • Workflow lock-in and file formats. PSD, AI, InDesign and PDF are working standards with decades of assets and trained users behind them. The numbers say the lock-in is holding: 97% subscription revenue, $22.2B of RPO, and 10% organic growth in a quarter when the stock was being priced for disruption.
  • Distribution at a scale rivals have to buy. Over 1 billion monthly active users across the business, 100M+ freemium creative users (+70% y/y), and Acrobat plus Express above 900M MAU (+25%). Adobe is now pushing Acrobat into ChatGPT, Chrome, Edge and WhatsApp, which means it is using the AI platforms’ distribution rather than being locked out of it.
  • Enterprise CX is a separate, sticky franchise. At Q1 three CX product lines each exceeded $1B of ARR and grew above 20% combined; in Q3 GenStudio, Experience Manager and Experience Platform each grew above 20%. This is the part of Adobe where AI agents plausibly increase the customer’s spend rather than replace the seat.
  • The P&L is the evidence. A 36–37% GAAP operating margin (ex-fee), ~40% FCF margin and capex under 1% of revenue are what pricing power looks like in the financials.

Where the moat is thinner than the bulls admit

  • Generative AI attacks the skill barrier, which was half the moat. A large part of why professionals paid for Photoshop was the learning curve and the sophistication of the tool. A prompt box inside a chat app, such as Anthropic’s Claude Design launched in April 2026, or a Canva or Figma workflow, lowers that barrier for the prosumer and SMB tier. Adobe is simultaneously a partner of OpenAI, Google and Anthropic and a company those partners can disrupt.
  • The AI revenue is small. AI-first ending ARR exceeded $650M, about 2.4% of $27.5B. Growing 150% is real, but my arithmetic puts it at roughly $0.4B of the ~$2.8B of ARR added over the year, about 14% of net new ARR. It has to carry more of the load if seat growth fades.
  • There is direct cannibalization in the numbers. Stock licensing, about $450M a year (1.7% of revenue), is described as declining, which is what a generative model does to a stock-image library.
  • Visibility has fallen. The disclosure change in FY26 (section 6) makes the creative-professional pool, the one most exposed to AI, harder to isolate.
  • Sentiment is already poor. Of 41 analysts, 25 rate it Hold and 5 Sell; the mean target is $269.50 (+10%), with a range of $195 to $373.

3. Growth drivers

Driver 1 — The core subscription franchise (proven, compounding)

Creative Cloud, Acrobat and Express are still the engine. Q3 revenue grew 13% reported and 12% in constant currency; Business Professionals & Consumers grew 16% and Creative & Marketing Professionals 13%. The headline overstates the organic rate. Semrush added about 2 points, so organic constant-currency growth was ~10%, and the Q4 guide ($6.80–6.85B) implies ~10.2% reported and only ~8% organic. The deceleration inside the guide is the first thing to check on December 9.

Sources: Adobe Q3 FY26 release and 10-Q; Q4 and FY26 guidance midpoints; Semrush quarterly revenue (~$120–125M) is my estimate from Semrush’s last public quarter ($117.7M in Q4 2025). Adobe says the Semrush contribution was not material and does not disclose it, so organic growth is derived, not reported. FX in Q4 and FY is shown as zero (the call summary cites only a “slight headwind”).

Driver 2 — Enterprise CX and GenStudio (proven, narrower)

Three CX lines above $1B of ARR each and a >20% growth rate for GenStudio, Experience Manager and Experience Platform make this the most defensible growth pool. It is also where the new CEO has spent his career, which is both a strength and the reason to watch whether creative gets the same attention.

Driver 3 — AI-first products (optionality, given partial credit)

Firefly ARR (above $250M at Q1, +40% sequentially in Q3), Acrobat AI Assistant (MAU doubled sequentially) and Brand Visibility (paid customers doubled sequentially) are real, but the total AI-first ARR is 2.4% of the base. I assign no acceleration to this driver in the base case: my 7.5% revenue CAGR is below the ~10% organic rate today, so the base case already assumes the core slows and AI does not fill the gap. Any AI upside is in the bull case only.

Driver 4 — Share shrink (financial, not operating)

Diluted shares have fallen from 481M in FY21 to a guided 400M in FY26, about 3% a year. That is worth ~3 points of annual EPS growth. I carry the same -3% in the base case and treat it as a capital-allocation choice, not as evidence of business momentum.

  • EPS growth is partly arithmetic. FY21–FY25 EPS compounded 13.6% against 10.3% for net income and 10.8% for revenue; the difference is the 11% fall in share count. FY25 EPS growth of +35% reported is ~+18% ex-fee (my estimate for the FY24 base).

Red flag worth its own paragraph: the metric that would settle the AI debate is no longer reported.

Through FY25, to my knowledge, Adobe reported Digital Media and Digital Experience segments and a Digital Media ARR figure. Beginning with FY26, the Q1 (March 12) and Q3 (September 10) releases report two customer groups and a single “Total Adobe ARR” ($26.06B at Q1, $27.50B at Q3); neither release gives a Digital Media ARR or net new ARR figure. The change was introduced in the same quarter that the CEO transition and the $25B buyback were announced. I allege nothing about motive, and I did not re-pull the FY25 releases to confirm the prior disclosure.

The practical effect is clear. The pool of customers most exposed to generative AI (creative professionals) is now blended with the pool least exposed (enterprise CX and Acrobat), and the new blended figure may include Semrush. AI-first ARR of $650M+ is a numerator with no denominator. Stripped of spin: you cannot currently observe the trend that bears and bulls are actually arguing about. That alone is a reason to hold a smaller position than the P&L would otherwise justify.

7. Valuation and five-year outlook

The setup. At $244.50, Adobe trades at 13.5x FY26 GAAP earnings, a 11.2% free-cash-flow yield and about 3.5x ARR. It is 33% below its high and 29% above its June 18 low of $190.12. It is cheaper than CRM and INTU on trailing earnings. What you are buying is a high-return franchise with a stable core and a disputed terminal value, with a 9.5% gross buyback yield behind it.

What is priced in at $244.50?

Holding margin at 35.5% and the share count shrinking 3% a year, a 10% annual return needs ~6.6% revenue growth if the stock still trades at 13x in 2031, or a 12.4x exit multiple if revenue grows 7.5%. If the multiple falls to the bear 9x, revenue growth would have to be ~14.7% to earn the hurdle. For break-even (0% return), revenue can shrink ~3% a year if the exit is 13x, but it must grow ~4.3% a year if the exit is 9x, so the multiple matters at least as much as growth. In plain terms: the market is pricing Adobe to grow at roughly the pace of the base case with no re-rating, which is a fair, not a distressed, expectation.

Fit with your portfolio’s growth objective

ADBE is a 35-share, ~$8.6K position (~0.50% of the portfolio, ~$8,156 cost, ~+5% as of today), built up from 16 shares in the October 6 export to 35 over ten days. It is not one of the five core forever holds, and the thesis it was bought on did not exist until this report. On your own screen of quality at a reasonable price and long-term compounding, ADBE passes quality (40% FCF margin, no net debt) and passes price, but fails the third test of a durable, unchallenged compounder, because the terminal value is the live debate. With a high risk profile and a 10-year horizon you can tolerate the dispersion in the scenario fan; the problem is that the expected value is at the hurdle, not above it.

Overlap. Application software already sits at ~4.9% of the portfolio (MSFT ~3.1%, CRM ~1.4%, ADBE ~0.5%) and all three turn on the same question of whether AI agents expand or compress seat-based software economics. ADBE adds to that bet more than it diversifies. There is no dividend, so tax location is not a factor. I have not re-run the other names in the October 8 capital-allocation ranking on these assumptions; compare ADBE’s 9.9% expected return against that ranking rather than treating this report as a standalone rank.

The three things to actually watch

  1. Organic growth and the FY27 guide (Dec 9). Q4 guide implies ~8% organic. A FY27 revenue guide that implies organic growth below ~7%, or margin guided below ~33%, moves me from the base to the bear case. A guide above ~9% organic with stable margin supports the bull weights.
  2. AI-first ARR in dollars, and whether creative ARR comes back. At $650M+ (2.4% of ARR) the question is whether it adds enough to offset any seat softening: I want to see it above ~$1.0B by mid-FY27, and I want Adobe to resume a creative or Digital Media ARR disclosure, which would remove the red flag.
  3. Leadership and capital discipline. Chakravarthy takes over December 1; a permanent CFO has not been named since June. Watch the pace and price of buybacks (does Adobe slow when the stock is above intrinsic value?) and whether the new team keeps GAAP margin at or above 35%.

Other dated items: Adobe MAX in November; Topaz Labs expected to close in Q4; Q1 FY27 results on or about March 11, 2027 (date tentative).

Bottom line

Adobe is a very good business at a fair price, not a very good business at a bargain price, and the difference is the AI question that the company has made harder to observe. My view is hold the starter, do not add at $244.50, and let price or evidence decide the next step. I would be more constructive at ~$222 (a 12% expected return on my weights, ~$195 for 15%), or at any price if two of the three watch items resolve favourably: organic growth holds near 10% through the CEO handoff, AI-first ARR scales past $1B, and a creative ARR figure is disclosed.

The reasoning is exposed so you can disagree with a step: the 13x clean multiple (stock comp counted), the ~8% organic Q4 run rate (Semrush stripped), the 7.5% base growth (below today’s 10%, with no AI acceleration), and the 30/50/20 weights. Move the weights to 25/40/35 and the expected return rises to 12.4%; cut the base-case exit multiple to 11x and the base IRR falls to +7.3%. This is a position to size to what you can hold through a bad outcome, not what the base case implies.
