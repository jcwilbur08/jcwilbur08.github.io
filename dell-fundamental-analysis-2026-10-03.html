Investment conclusion

Dell is no longer a PC company that happens to sell servers — it is a top-tier AI-infrastructure vendor with a $95 billion order book, and the September quarter answered the single biggest bear question by delivering a record 15.0% Infrastructure Solutions Group operating margin despite heavy AI-server mix. Full-year guidance was raised dramatically: revenue to $192 billion and non-GAAP EPS to $25.50. But the stock has run 347% year-to-date and now trades at about 21.6x FY2027 consensus earnings, sitting at the sell-side consensus target and roughly 15% above Morningstar's $490 fair value, which carries no economic moat. The business deserves respect; the price deserves patience. Initiate HOLD with a $600 twelve-month target (about 7% upside). Add only on weakness, at or below $500.

1. The quarter: a $95 billion backlog blowout

Q2 FY2027 — reported September 1, 2026 · quarter ended July 31

Dell reported the strongest quarter in company history. Revenue of $46.97 billion rose 58% year over year and beat the roughly $44.9 billion consensus. Non-GAAP EPS of $7.04 was up 203% and beat consensus of about $4.91 by $2.13; GAAP EPS of $6.34 rose 273% on $4.13 billion of net income. Infrastructure Solutions Group revenue of $31.78 billion grew 89%, with AI-optimized servers doubling to a record $16.4 billion, traditional servers and networking up 122% to $10.53 billion, and storage up 26% to a record $4.9 billion. ISG operating income of $4.78 billion rose 225%, and the 15.0% margin — up from 8.8% a year earlier and 10.5% in Q1 — is the number that matters most in this report, because it directly refutes the margin-dilution bear case that has capped Dell's multiple. Client Solutions Group was no drag either: revenue of $15.03 billion rose 20%, with record commercial client revenue of $13.2 billion up 22% and consumer up 7%; operating income rose 42% to $1.1 billion.

The order book is the real story. AI-server orders of $60.9 billion were a record, up from $24.4 billion in Q1, against quarterly shipments of $16.4 billion — a 3.7x book-to-ship ratio. The AI backlog nearly doubled in one quarter, from $51.3 billion to $95 billion, which covers about 128% of the full-year AI-server revenue guidance and represents nearly six quarters of shipments at the current run rate. Trailing-twelve-month AI orders reached $131.7 billion across more than 6,500 customers. Dell-defined adjusted free cash flow was $8.15 billion, up 224%, and the company returned a record $4.3 billion to shareholders in the quarter.

Management raised full-year guidance aggressively: revenue up $25 billion to $192 billion (about 69% growth), AI-server revenue guidance raised from $60 billion to $74 billion (about 200% growth), GAAP EPS of $24.37 (up 181%), and non-GAAP EPS of $25.50, up from the prior $17.90 guide. For Q3, management guided revenue of $49.0 billion (up 81%) and non-GAAP EPS of $6.50. Note the implied shape: first-half revenue was about $87 billion, so the back half must deliver roughly $105 billion — the backlog makes it credible, but conversion has to be near-flawless.

2. The AI-server franchise: orders, customers, and the Rubin ramp

$131.7B trailing orders · 6,500+ AI customers · first to ship Vera Rubin

Dell's AI franchise is broad-based, not a single-customer story. Reported customers include neoclouds CoreWeave and Nscale, plus xAI, for which Dell supplied tens of thousands of GPU servers for the Colossus supercomputer in Memphis. On June 1, 2026, Dell announced it was the first vendor to ship NVIDIA Vera Rubin NVL72 systems — PowerRack systems with PowerEdge XE9812 servers delivered to CoreWeave, which completed operational validation the same day — following its early Grace Blackwell NVL72 rack shipments in Q2. It has since launched the liquid-cooled PowerEdge XE8812 (Vera Rubin NVL4, up to 144 GPUs per rack) as the centerpiece of the Dell AI Factory with NVIDIA. NVIDIA expects Vera Rubin to reach full production by mid-2026, with deployments also planned at the major hyperscalers. Dell's AI customer base has grown by more than 3,300 in three quarters, spanning neoclouds, sovereigns, and enterprises, and management describes the pipeline as several times the size of the backlog.

Competitively, Dell holds top-tier server share and trades at a premium to peers: about 18x next-twelve-months earnings at the post-earnings price versus roughly 12.6x for HPE and 8.1x for Super Micro. Super Micro disclosed more than $60 billion of new orders and guided FY2027 revenue of $65–72 billion, but with a far smaller market cap and open questions about cash conversion. The premium is defensible on backlog visibility and execution, but it prices in a continuation of the current order cadence.

What changed our mind on the bear case: AI servers carry structurally lower margins — GPUs, HBM, and power systems dominate the bill of materials, and NVIDIA captures most of the value — and management's own target for AI-server operating margins is mid-single digits. Yet blended ISG margin hit a record 15.0% this quarter. The drivers: Dell passed through DRAM and component inflation with frequent repricing, traditional servers grew even faster (up 122%) at richer margins, storage — the most profitable line — grew 26%, and opex leverage kicked in. The margin story is real, but we should be clear about what it depends on: continued pricing power in an inflationary component environment.

3. The margin question: answered this quarter, structurally still thin

Mid-single-digit AI margins · gross margin whipsaw 18.1% → 21.1% · cash lags profit

The structural tension has not disappeared. As AI servers approach and exceed half of ISG revenue, mix alone pushes blended margins down; the defense is pricing power, richer traditional-server and storage mix, and opex discipline — all of which have to hold simultaneously. Gross margin whipsawed from 18.1% in Q1 FY2027 to about 21.1% in Q2, and management named the pressure points: DRAM, NAND, CPUs, disk drives, optical components, and substrates. That volatility is the cost of the current model.

Cash conversion is the honest caveat. GAAP free cash flow was only $986 million in Q2 against $4.13 billion of net income — inventory doubled since January to $21.3 billion and Dell Financial Services financing receivables reached $20.4 billion on a 213% surge in originations. Growth of this speed consumes working capital; it is a feature of the backlog, not a fraud signal, but it means earnings quality runs behind earnings quantity until the backlog converts and inventories normalize. One third-party report pegs EV/EBITDA at 21.8x and free-cash-flow yield at 2.2% — both are approximate, from a single source, and should be treated as directional rather than precise.

4. The other businesses: PCs, storage, and services

Servers now exceed PCs · storage is the margin gem · services attach undisclosed

For context on the transformation: in FY2026, ISG revenue of $60.8 billion (up 40%) overtook CSG revenue of $51.0 billion (up 5.4%) — servers are now Dell's largest revenue source. CSG itself has returned to growth, and the Windows 10 end-of-support (October 14, 2025) drove a drawn-out commercial refresh that management expected to extend into calendar 2026. But the caution is explicit: Windows 11 adoption was running 10–12 points behind Windows 10's pace at the same stage, and Dell visibly scaled back its "AI PC" branding push at CES 2026, refocusing on gaming, build quality, battery life, and displays. Consumer PCs remain weak at $1.8 billion of quarterly revenue. The PC tail is a 2026 story with limited visibility beyond it.

Storage is the quiet gem: $4.9 billion of quarterly revenue, up 26%, a record for a second quarter, and Dell's most profitable line — AI workloads are pulling storage attach, and the Dell AI Factory model bundles servers with PowerScale and PowerStore storage, networking, integration, and deployment services. The one gap we could not close: Dell does not disclose services-attach rates, so the margin-accretive attach narrative is directionally right but unquantified.

5. Capital allocation: dividends, buybacks, and a fortress balance sheet

$2.52 annualized dividend · $10B buyback authorization · 0.8x core leverage

Dell returns about 50% of free cash flow to shareholders, and the trajectory is aggressive. The annual dividend was raised 18% to $2.10 in February 2026 — the third consecutive double-digit increase — and then declared at $0.63 quarterly ($2.52 annualized, a further 20% step) on September 1, payable October 30 with an October 20 ex-div date. At $562.52 that yields only about 0.45% — this is a growth story, not an income story. The board added $10 billion to the repurchase authorization in February; in Q2 Dell repurchased 9.5 million shares at an average $401, diluted shares fell 5% year over year to 652 million, and the company returned $4.3 billion in the quarter and $6.3 billion in the first half.

The balance sheet is under no strain: core leverage of 0.8x sits far below the roughly 2.5x target set after the VMware spin, and rating agencies took positive actions in Q2. One honest note on the optics: capital returns have been partly debt-funded — total debt principal rose $6.7 billion during the first nine months of FY2026 alongside accelerated buybacks, and Dell International/EMC completed a $5 billion senior-notes offering across four tranches on September 15, 2026. Cash stands at about $14.2 billion. The leverage is cheap and the returns are enormous; it is worth noting, not fearing.

6. Valuation and target framework

~21.6x FY2027 consensus · at the consensus target · above Morningstar FVE

At $562.52, Dell trades at about 21.6x FY2027 consensus EPS of $26.09 (our calculation) and roughly 30–33x trailing earnings. The consensus 12-month target averages about $567 — essentially the current price — with a wide $465–$700 range and a street high of $735. Morningstar raised fair value to $490 after the quarter, rates the shares 3 stars, and assigns no economic moat: the market is about 15% above their estimate of intrinsic value. Consensus FY2028 calls for EPS of $30.24 on revenue of $230.9 billion, about 16% EPS growth.

$600 twelve-month target (about 7% upside). Framework: 23x FY2027 consensus EPS of $26.09, a premium multiple justified by the $95 billion backlog, 200% AI-server growth, and record margins — but a premium for a no-moat hardware franchise with mid-single-digit AI margins cannot stretch much further. Cross-checks: about 19.8x FY2028 consensus EPS of $30.24; the consensus average target of $567 (about 1% upside) and Morningstar's $490 fair value (about 13% below the price) bracket our target from below; RBC's $640 and Melius's $735 bracket it from above. We underwrite the backlog at a full-but-not-extreme multiple, not a fourth AI order wave.

7. Portfolio fit and recommended gate

New coverage · the downstream AI layer · stage on weakness

Dell is not held, and it should be framed honestly against what the book already owns. The portfolio has AI-compute exposure through NVIDIA, Broadcom, and TSMC — the picks, the networking, and the fab. Dell is the downstream box-maker: it buys NVIDIA's GPUs, assembles the racks, and earns mid-single-digit margins doing it, while tying up $21 billion of inventory and $20 billion of financing receivables. It is the same AI capex wave at the thinnest margin and the highest working-capital intensity in the stack — and, on the other side, the only layer where a $95 billion backlog is directly visible on the books. There is no moat here by Morningstar's reckoning; there is a two-year order book. Own it for the backlog, not the franchise.

Recommended gate: DELL ADD at or below $500 toward ~1% of NAV. At $500 the forward multiple compresses to about 19.2x FY2027 consensus EPS, the entry sits just above Morningstar's $490 fair value and about 11% below the current price — a fair price for a no-moat cyclical riding the strongest order book in its history. Size the starter toward ~1% of NAV (about 34 shares / roughly $17,000 at the gate). Break below $465 — the low end of the sell-side target range — on fundamental news means reassess, not buy. Gate recommendation only — not installed in the dashboard panel pending CIO approval.

Decision posture

Rating: HOLD (initiation). The backlog is real and the quarter was superb, but the price has caught up: 347% year-to-date, at the consensus target, above Morningstar fair value.
What would upgrade it: a pullback to the $500 gate, or evidence that ISG margins hold above 13% through the Rubin ramp — either de-risks the entry.
What would downgrade it: backlog conversion slippage, AI-server margin reversion toward mid-single digits, or a neocloud financing event that strands orders.
Positioning vs. the book: complements NVDA/AVGO/TSM as the visible-backlog downstream layer; do not size it as a second NVIDIA.

8. Risks and catalysts into year-end

Concentration, cash, and components are the watch list

Risks: (1) AI-server margin dilution — management's own mid-single-digit target is the ceiling; as AI exceeds half of ISG revenue, mix compresses blended margins unless pricing power holds. (2) Customer concentration and AI-capex vulnerability — a small cluster of hyperscale and neocloud customers drives the bulk of AI revenue, and Dell does not disclose concentration; neocloud financing strain is visible elsewhere in the ecosystem. (3) PC cyclicality — the Windows 10 end-of-life refresh tail is likely ending, Windows 11 adoption lags the prior cycle's pace, and AI PCs underwhelmed. (4) Component inflation — DRAM, NAND, CPUs, and substrates whipsawed gross margin from 18.1% to 21.1% in a single quarter. (5) Working capital — $21.3 billion of inventory and $20.4 billion of financing receivables mean cash trails profit until the backlog converts. (6) Valuation — up 347% year-to-date at 21.6x forward earnings, expectations are demanding; insiders sold heavily into the run, including more than $5 billion of insider sales over the past year by one account.

Catalysts: Q3 FY2027 earnings, estimated November 24 after close (a provider projection, not yet company-confirmed; management guided $49.0 billion revenue and $6.50 non-GAAP EPS); the $0.63 dividend payable October 30 (ex-div October 20); continued neocloud and hyperscaler order announcements, including the October 1 framework memorandum with JERA and RHAELM on AI infrastructure in Japan (prospective, not booked revenue); and the NVIDIA Vera Rubin production ramp through mid-2026.
