§0 — The one-paragraph version

Uber's stock has fallen 23% over the past year and now sits 31% below its 52-week high, even as the business has never been stronger: GAAP operating income roughly quintupled from $1.1B to $5.6B in two years, Adjusted EBITDA margin hit 17%, and free cash flow is up 7% year-over-year on essentially no capital intensity. The market is pricing in caution about two real things — a Waymo robotaxi threat that's now genuinely commercial, and a $14.8B all-cash-and-debt takeover bid for Delivery Hero that won't close until H2 2027 — while apparently discounting a headline P/E of 15.5x that is itself a mirage: half of last year's reported earnings came from a one-time Dutch tax-asset release, and the "clean" multiple is closer to 28-29x. Strip the noise both ways and you get a business compounding operating income at 100%+/yr trading at a price that, on our numbers, only needs mid-teens Adjusted EBITDA growth — well below its recent trend — to clear a 10% annual return. The crux isn't whether Uber's marketplace works today; it's whether Waymo's own app increasingly wins riders directly instead of riding on Uber's platform, and whether the Delivery Hero integration justifies the debt taken on to fund it.

1. History, business model, and revenue mix

The short history

Uber went public in 2019 having spent a decade burning cash to buy market share in ride-hailing, then food delivery. The story since has been a slow, then sudden, march to profitability: a $9.1B GAAP net loss in FY2022 (driven heavily by write-downs on equity stakes acquired in prior divestitures — Aurora, Didi, Grab — during that year's tech-stock rout), followed by the company's first full GAAP-profitable year in FY2023, and an operating-income trajectory that has since compounded at roughly 100% a year. Dara Khosrowshahi, CEO since 2017, has pursued a consistent playbook: exit markets Uber can't win outright (selling operations to local leaders in exchange for equity stakes — the source of much of the mark-to-market noise discussed in §5), build a multi-sided marketplace around Mobility and Delivery, and more recently, partner rather than build for autonomous vehicles.

Business model

Uber takes a share of Gross Bookings (the total dollar value transacted on the platform) as revenue, netting out driver/courier earnings and various platform-participant incentives. The economics are genuinely asset-light — capital expenditures were just $135M in H1 2026 against $27.4B of revenue — because Uber owns none of the vehicles, restaurants, or trucks on its platform. The moat, such as it is, comes from two-sided network effects (more riders attract more drivers, which shortens wait times, which attracts more riders) reinforced by the Uber One membership program and a fast-growing advertising business layered on top of both Mobility and Delivery.

The mix story

Delivery has gone from 33% to 37% of the Mobility+Delivery total in two years and its margin has nearly doubled (12.3% → 20.7%), the clearest evidence that Uber Eats has crossed from subsidized growth to genuine scale economics. Freight, by contrast, has been dead money for three straight years — barely 10% of revenue and consistently loss-making at the Adjusted EBITDA line, a drag management has not meaningfully addressed. Geographically, EMEA is now the fastest-growing region (+30.6% YoY to $16.4B, 31.5% of revenue) — directly relevant given the Delivery Hero deal is a bet on doubling down there.

2. Moat and competitive landscape

Where the moat is real

  • Two-sided network density in Mobility and Delivery. 202 million Monthly Active Platform Consumers (Q4 2025, +18% YoY) and 3.9 billion trips in Q2 2026 alone (+18% YoY) — scale that's genuinely hard to replicate market-by-market, especially against a well-capitalized but much smaller Lyft ($5.7B market cap vs. Uber's $144.0B).
  • Uber One membership and advertising both sit on top of the core marketplace and improve unit economics without proportional cost — the mechanism behind the steady Adjusted EBITDA margin expansion in both segments.
  • A deliberately capital-light AV strategy. Rather than building autonomous vehicle technology itself, Uber has struck partnerships across the industry — WeRide (Spain's first Level 4 permit, granted this week, 15 planned cities by 2030), and a Tokyo launch planned for late 2026 via Hinomaru Kotsu — spreading technology risk across multiple partners instead of betting the company on one.

Where the moat is thinner than the bulls admit

  • Waymo is no longer just a partner — it's a direct, commercial competitor, and it is scaling fast. Waymo runs its own rider app in multiple U.S. cities (expanding into Las Vegas this month) in addition to supplying vehicles onto Uber's platform in some markets. Uber's own 10-Q says the quiet part directly: its AV partners "could remove their vehicles from our platform," and if a competitor's autonomous technology proves cheaper or is perceived as safer, "our financial performance and prospects would be adversely impacted." Waymo's own Tokyo ambitions (unsupervised launch targeted 2027) put it on a collision course with Uber's separate Tokyo AV plans in the same city.
  • Driver classification risk hasn't gone away — it's evolved. California and Massachusetts have both moved toward driver unionization frameworks (Gov. Newsom signed enabling legislation in 2025), and the underlying "employee vs. independent contractor" litigation is still live in multiple jurisdictions. A reclassification anywhere large would not be a modest cost increase — Uber's own filings call it a fundamental change to the business model.
  • The Freight segment is proof the marketplace model doesn't automatically work everywhere. Three years of sub-scale, loss-making results suggest network effects in freight brokerage are weaker than in Mobility/Delivery, or that Uber simply lacks the density to compete with incumbents there.

3. Growth drivers

1
Delivery margin scaling (proven, compounding). Delivery Adjusted EBITDA margin has gone from 12.3% to 20.7% in two years as Uber Eats crosses into density-driven profitability. If this continues toward Mobility's ~27% margin, Delivery alone could add well over $1B of incremental Adjusted EBITDA without needing much revenue growth at all.
2
Advertising and Uber One (proven, compounding). Both are described by the company as embedded in Mobility and Delivery revenue rather than broken out separately, but they are the structural reason segment margins keep climbing even as Gross Bookings growth decelerates from its post-pandemic pace — a higher-margin layer on top of the core marketplace.
3
The Delivery Hero acquisition (proven event, unproven integration). On July 16, 2026, Uber agreed to acquire Delivery Hero for €41.50/share cash (€14.8B/$14.8B equity value for 100%), funded by existing cash plus a €14.2B bridge facility already partly refinanced by the €4.5B of senior notes issued September 15, 2026. The deal — subject to a 50%+1 acceptance threshold and merger-control/financial-regulatory clearances — won't close until H2 2027, an unusually long timeline that itself is a risk (see §6). If it closes on the terms disclosed, it meaningfully extends Uber's European and Asian Delivery density, the same region already growing fastest organically (EMEA +30.6% YoY).
4
Autonomous vehicles as a cost-reduction lever (optionality, double-edged). If Uber's AV partnerships scale successfully, removing driver payouts from a growing share of trips could structurally expand Mobility margins well beyond today's 26.6%. But this is the same lever Waymo can pull for itself by disintermediating Uber entirely — we assign this zero net weight in the base case given the driver cuts both ways, and treat any Uber-specific AV margin benefit as bull-case optionality only.

The strip-the-noise finding: half of FY2025's headline EPS is a one-time, non-cash item the company discloses explicitly. Of the $10.1B in FY2025 net income, $5.0B — 49.7% — is a one-time benefit from releasing a valuation allowance against Uber's Netherlands deferred tax assets, a non-cash accounting event with no bearing on operating performance. Strip it out and clean net income is $5.05B, clean EPS is $2.47 (not the headline $4.92), and the "cheap" 15.5x P/E becomes a 28.5x clean P/E — still reasonable given the operating-income growth rate, but a materially different starting point than the headline suggests. This is not a one-off pattern either: Q2 FY2026's $2.4B net income included roughly $1.6B of pre-tax unrealized gains on equity stakes (Delivery Hero +$1.1B, Aurora +$899M, Didi -$437M) — close to two-thirds of that quarter's headline earnings came from marks on strategic investments, not the marketplace business. Any analysis using trailing GAAP EPS for Uber without adjusting for this is measuring noise, not signal.

Bear case vs. bull case

Bull case

  • Delivery margin keeps climbing toward Mobility's level as Uber Eats scales, adding well over $1B of incremental Adjusted EBITDA
  • Delivery Hero closes on schedule and integrates cleanly, extending European/Asian Delivery density meaningfully
  • Uber's multi-partner AV strategy proves the right hedge — WeRide, and other partners scale across the 15 planned cities without Waymo displacing Uber's platform access
  • The buyback continues at pace ($15.7B remaining) while operating income keeps compounding, driving strong per-share value growth on top of business growth
  • Result modeled: +31.5%/yr, five-year exit near $277

Bear case

  • Waymo's own-app expansion accelerates faster than its Uber-platform partnership, and other AV players (Tesla, Zoox) follow the same playbook in additional cities
  • Delivery Hero integration proves harder or more expensive than expected, or the deal falls through after Uber has already carried the debt and bridge-financing costs for over a year
  • A driver-classification ruling or new state legislation forces a costly change to the independent-contractor model in a large market
  • The common-carrier liability theory from the September 2026 arbitration spreads to other jurisdictions, raising Uber's effective insurance/liability cost structure
  • Result modeled: -1.9%/yr, five-year exit near $64 — roughly flat, not a severe loss, but a real underperformance against any reasonable opportunity cost

7. Valuation and five-year outlook

The setup. At $70.51, Uber trades at a headline 15.5x P/E that overstates the bargain (see §5's clean 28.5x figure) but at a reasonable EV/EBITDA multiple (roughly 16.5x trailing, using today's market cap against FY25 Adjusted EBITDA of $8.7B) for a business whose Adjusted EBITDA has grown 35-60%/yr for two straight years and whose stock has nonetheless fallen 23% over the past twelve months — a genuine expectations gap worth interrogating rather than a red flag on its own.

What is priced in at $70.51?

Holding the base case's exit multiple (14x, itself a compression from today's implied ~16.5x) fixed, Uber needs only a 12% five-year Adjusted EBITDA CAGR to clear a 10% annual return — reaching roughly $15.4B of FY31 Adjusted EBITDA versus FY25's $8.7B. That is a materially lower bar than the 35-60%/yr growth Adjusted EBITDA has actually posted over the last two years, and even our own conservative Base case (18%) clears it with room to spare. The honest reframe: at today's price, you are not betting that Uber's growth reaccelerates or even sustains its recent pace — you are betting that growth merely decelerates to something well short of "good," which is a lower bar than the market's 23% one-year decline in the stock seems to imply.

Fit with your portfolio's growth objective

Uber pays no dividend, so the yield-irrelevance framing that applies across this portfolio is moot here by default — total return is the only lens that matters, and that's the right lens for this name regardless. UBER is not currently a holding, and unlike a name that would concentrate the AI-infrastructure bet already running through AVGO, MSFT, SOXQ, and CRM, this is a genuinely different economic driver: consumer mobility and delivery demand, urban density trends, and AV-driven disruption risk — none of which correlates tightly with data-center capex cycles. That makes it a cleaner diversification candidate than most "different" pitches this portfolio sees, worth weighing on its own merits rather than as a correlation hedge alone.

What to monitor next

The three things to actually watch

1
Waymo's own-app city expansion pace vs. its Uber-platform partnership cities. Every new Waymo market is either additive to Uber (a partnership city) or a direct competitive loss (an own-app-only city) — track which category each announcement falls into, starting with the Las Vegas and Tokyo rollouts already underway.
2
Delivery Hero deal progress toward the H2 2027 close. Watch for regulatory clearance milestones and the acceptance-threshold outcome; a delay or a failed deal after debt has already been issued would be a negative catalyst worth re-underwriting around.
3
Adjusted EBITDA margin trend, especially in Delivery. Currently 20.7% and closing on Mobility's 26.6% — continued convergence is the clearest evidence the bull case's margin-expansion driver is real and on track; a stall would be the first sign the base case's 18% CAGR assumption is at risk.

Bottom line

Uber's operating business has quietly become one of the better growth-and-margin stories in large-cap consumer tech — GAAP operating income up roughly 5x in two years, Adjusted EBITDA margin at 17% and climbing, free cash flow growing on essentially no capital intensity — while the stock has fallen 23% over the same twelve months. Some of that gap is noise (a headline P/E flattered by a one-time tax item that cuts the other way once you strip it out), and some of it is real: Waymo's transition from AV technology partner to genuine commercial competitor is not fully resolved, and the Delivery Hero acquisition adds real execution and leverage risk on a long, 13-month-plus timeline.

Netting it out: the price does not require heroics to work. A 12% five-year Adjusted EBITDA CAGR clears a 10% return — a bar this business has cleared by a wide margin in each of the last two years — and even a genuinely bad outcome (Waymo disintermediation plus a rocky Delivery Hero integration) models out closer to flat than to a severe capital loss. For a holder with a 10-year horizon and a pension floor, this is a name whose risk is concentrated in two specific, observable, dated questions rather than diffused across the whole thesis — which makes it easier to monitor and to size than most names carrying a similar expected return.
