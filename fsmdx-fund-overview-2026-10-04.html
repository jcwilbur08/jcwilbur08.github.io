Executive Summary

Investment conclusion

FSMDX is the mid-cap sibling of FSSNX, and if anything the better of the two. A 4-star fund tracking the Russell Midcap Index at 0.025% with roughly $53 billion in assets and tracking error of about 0.05% - replication so tight that in the first quarter of 2026 the fund returned 1.30% against the index's 1.29%. September was the segment's stress test (the Russell Midcap fell hard as the Fed hiked to 3.75-4.00%), yet FSMDX finished the month at $41.93, up roughly 13-15% year-to-date on a total-return basis.

Research posture: Hold | Conviction 6/10 | No price target (index vehicle)

This is the canonical "higher conviction with insufficient upside" case in the new Hold definition. Conviction in the vehicle is a genuine 6 - among the cheapest, tightest-tracking mid-cap index funds in existence, run by Fidelity's tenured index desk since 2011. But the second prong of the scale - expected upside - is approximately zero by design: an index fund's job is to match its benchmark, not beat it. The honest forward statement is that FSMDX should deliver the Russell Midcap's total return minus ~2.5 basis points of fees, nothing more.

Objective

What the fund owns and why

FSMDX seeks results that correspond to the total return of U.S. mid-cap stocks, investing at least 80% of assets in the Russell Midcap Index - the bottom 800 stocks of the Russell 1000, i.e., companies ranked 201 through 1,000 by market capitalization. It is market-cap weighted, reconstituted annually by FTSE Russell, and crucially has no profitability screen: a mid-cap company is included on size alone, profitable or not.

The Russell Midcap is the default institutional mid-cap benchmark - broader than the S&P 400 and constructed purely on size. Its top end reaches into what many would call large caps and its bottom end bleeds into small-cap territory, which is why it behaves as a smoother bridge between the two segments.

September was the stress test. The Fed raised 25 bps to 3.75-4.00% (the first hike since 2023) and the 10-year pushed toward 5.3%; mid caps drew down ~4%+ in the month and Q3 finished around -7.5% for small/mid segments. FSMDX's decline matched the index almost to the basis point - exactly the behavior a well-run index fund is supposed to produce, and exactly why the rating rests on the vehicle, not the month.

One honest footnote on the mid-cap segment: over 10 years FSMDX's ~11.3-11.7% annualized has trailed the S&P 500's ~13.4% - the large-cap decade is visible in every size-segment comparison. Mid caps are held for diversification and for their tendency to lead in broad-market recoveries, not because they beat large caps every decade.

The indexes genuinely differ: the Russell Midcap is the bottom 800 of the Russell 1000 - pure market-cap ranking with no quality filter. The S&P MidCap 400 is a committee-selected 400 with an earnings requirement, which tilts it toward established earners. But "different index" is not the same as "diversification": Russell Midcap and S&P MidCap 400 move together - both are U.S. mid-cap beta with heavy membership overlap. Neither position is big enough to be a bet, and the smaller duplicate position costs twice as much.

Analyst recommendation: Hold both where they sit (advisory)

FSMDX stays as the mid-cap core in its account at 0.025% - broader (805 vs 400 stocks), cheaper, and better-rated than IJH. The cross-account consolidation is not available: FSMDX and IJH sit in different retirement accounts, so each account keeps its own mid-cap vehicle. The overlap is a venial cross-account redundancy, not an actionable one; the case would reopen only if the accounts are ever combined. The consolidated ~3% mid-cap weight sits comfortably between the large-cap-dominated core and the ~4.5% small-cap sleeve - a textbook size-segment ladder. No trim or add is needed on the segment; the fix is structural, not sizing.

Sizing judgment

At 1.83%, FSMDX is a right-sized core exposure for a $1.7M retirement book. No accumulation gate is recommended for an index vehicle - if the mid-cap sleeve drifts materially above ~3.5% of NAV on a segment run, direct new dollars to the large-cap or international sleeves rather than the index. There is no active sell case: the fund is cheap, tight-tracking, and full-sized.

Risks - what could change the verdict

  • Mid-cap cycle risk. The risk is the segment, not the fund: mid caps are economically sensitive and underperform in narrow, mega-cap-led rallies. The 10-year shortfall vs the S&P 500 is the visible cost of that cyclicality.
  • Rate sensitivity. Mid caps carry more leverage and floating-rate exposure than large caps; the September hike and signaled further tightening are a headwind for index earnings. The October FOMC is the checkpoint.
  • Tracking breakdown. Would matter only if tracking error widened materially - it runs at ~0.05%. No concern.
  • Fee increase or benchmark change. Not a real risk - Fidelity uses these funds as franchise builders.
  • Index methodology shifts. FTSE Russell periodically adjusts reconstitution rules; a material change in the Midcap band definition would alter what the fund owns. Nothing is on the table.

Appendix B - Glossary

NAV - net asset value: a mutual fund's per-share price, set once daily after market close. Expense ratio - annual operating cost as a percentage of assets; 0.025% = $2.50 per $10,000 per year. Tracking error - how closely a fund follows its index; smaller is better. Russell Midcap - the bottom 800 stocks of the Russell 1000 (ranks 201-1,000 by market cap); the default institutional mid-cap benchmark. S&P MidCap 400 - 400 committee-selected U.S. mid-cap companies with a profitability requirement. Securities lending - the fund lends portfolio stocks to short-sellers for a fee, offsetting expenses. Reconstitution - the index's annual refresh of membership, which index funds must trade through.
