1. Executive Summary & Investment Thesis

PepsiCo is the hardest call in the staples sleeve, and that is exactly why it is interesting. The second quarter was a study in divergence: headline revenue beat at $24.18B (+6.4%), core EPS of $2.20 missed by a penny, and the composition underneath was ugly — North American beverage volume fell 4% while revenue rose 7% on acquisitions, and the North American foods business traded price for volume and got neither: Frito-Lay/Quaker volume went flat after price cuts of up to 15%, with segment revenue down 2% and operating profit down 3.5%. Management itself now calls the recovery "more gradual." The stock sits within a dollar of its 52-week low at $128.63, down ~10% year-to-date while Coca-Cola is up 26%.

And yet. The business underneath the North American noise is a wide-moat global compounder: international organic revenue grew 7%, EMEA core operating profit rose 29%, Asia Pacific foods grew 35%, and global organic volume ran at its best rate since 2022. The balance sheet supports a 54th consecutive dividend increase at a ~4.6% yield, and activist Elliott Investment Management's ~$4B stake is forcing the exact medicine the business needs — ~20% SKU rationalization, bottling-plant closures, and a North America supply-chain review due late 2026. At ~15× forward core earnings — the low end of the five-year range, down from 26× at the 2021 peak — the market is pricing the bear case. Morningstar agrees: 5 stars, $169 fair value.

Bottom line: BUY with a $152 12-month target — ~18% price upside plus a ~4.6% dividend yield for a ~23% total return. The target is anchored on 17.5× estimated 2026 core EPS of ~$8.65, a partial re-rating from today's ~15× toward the high-teens the business has historically commanded — full re-rating to 20×+ requires the North America volume inflection, which is not yet in evidence. This is a contrarian, paid-to-wait position: the dividend funds the patience, Elliott forces the execution, and international growth covers the wait. The honest caveat is real — this can be a value trap if volumes keep sliding — which is why sizing stays measured until Q3 proves the turn.
What would change the call: the BUY rests on three testable propositions — (1) North America volume stabilizes by Q4, (2) the Elliott restructuring delivers the late-2026 supply-chain review with real cost takeout, and (3) core operating margin stops contracting. If Q3 (October 8) shows volumes deteriorating further or management trims the 4–6% core EPS guide, the rating goes to HOLD regardless of how cheap the multiple looks. Cheap can get cheaper; only execution earns the re-rating.

2. Business Overview

PepsiCo, headquartered in Purchase, New York, generated nearly $94B in net revenue in 2025, making it the world's largest convenient-foods-and-beverages company by sales. The portfolio is uniquely balanced: salty snacks (Lay's, Doritos, Cheetos, Tostitos), beverages (Pepsi, Mountain Dew, Gatorade), and foods (Quaker) — with convenient foods historically contributing the majority of operating profit. Products are consumed more than a billion times a day in 200+ countries and territories. The structural asset no competitor can replicate is the direct-store-delivery (DSD) network behind Frito-Lay in North America — the shelf-space machine that makes PepsiCo simultaneously #1 in US salty snacks and a top-two US beverage player.

4. Segment Analysis

Frito-Lay North America — the watch item

FLNA is the segment this entire investment case hinges on, and Q2 was discouraging. Revenue declined 2%, volume was flat, and operating profit fell 3.5% — this after the company cut suggested prices on major US snack brands by up to nearly 15% earlier in the year. In Q1 those cuts had driven a 2% volume increase, the first growth in over two years; in Q2 the volume response stalled at zero while the price giveback flowed straight through to revenue and profit. Volumes at the North American food business have now fallen in four of the last six quarters. The consumer verdict on the affordability program is in: price alone is not bringing the volume back.

PepsiCo Beverages North America — volume down, revenue up

PBNA revenue rose 7%, but unit volume fell 4% — the growth came from acquisitions and divestitures (contributing about six points), not from selling more drinks. Worse, the operating margin contracted 90 basis points, dragged by commercial arrangements tied to the Alani energy-drink partnership and softness in the convenience channel. Both carbonated and non-carbonated beverages are under pressure. This is the mirror image of Coca-Cola's North America, where volume grew 3% in the same quarter.

International — the engine that works

Everything outside North America is performing. International organic revenue grew 7%; EMEA core operating profit increased 29%; Asia Pacific foods volume grew 10% with APAC food business growth of 35%; International Beverage Franchise volume rose 5%. The international business is not a side note — it is carrying the consolidated growth story while North America restructures.

Quaker Foods — quiet recovery

Quaker, dented by the 2024 product recalls, is stabilizing as part of the North American foods portfolio. The Q1 return to volume growth across Frito-Lay and Quaker showed the brands retain consumer permission; the Q2 stall was a category-demand problem, not a Quaker-specific one. Quaker remains a turnaround contributor rather than a growth driver for 2026.

5. Pricing Power: the Failed Elasticity Experiment

PepsiCo spent the inflation era taking price aggressively — and trained a generation of shoppers to check the private-label price first. When volumes cracked, management reversed course with blanket wholesale price cuts of up to 15% on Lay's, Doritos, Cheetos, and Tostitos. The Q2 result is the empirical answer to whether that works: unit volume stayed flat at 0% while net revenue fell 2%. The price elasticity proved asymmetric — consumers who defected to Walmart's Great Value and Kroger private labels, priced 47–63% below branded salty snacks, did not come back for a 15% cut.

This is the central strategic lesson of the quarter and the core of the Elliott thesis: PepsiCo cannot price-cut its way back to growth. What it needs is price-pack architecture — smaller packs at sharper absolute price points, better-for-you premium tiers, and SKU rationalization (~20% catalog cut) that focuses investment on brands with pricing power — rather than list-price volatility. Management has begun this pivot; the Q3 and Q4 prints will show whether it is executing or just describing.

6. Volume Trends

Volume is bifurcated by geography, and the bifurcation defines the risk. Globally, organic volume is running at its best rate since 2022 — foods +3%, beverages +2% — and the North American convenient-foods business gained volume market share even in a flat quarter. But North America, the highest-margin market, is the drag: NA beverage volume −4%, NA foods volume flat, and management guiding to a "more gradual" recovery.

Three structural headwinds bear on the volume outlook. First, GLP-1 weight-loss drugs — now used in roughly one in five American households — are a slow-burn demand question for salty snacks specifically. Second, the broader shift toward healthier eating and protein-forward snacking favors PepsiCo's better-for-you portfolio (which it is building via acquisition) over its core salty-snack base. Third, stretched lower-income consumers are trading down to private label, a share loss that is sticky even when branded prices fall. None of these is fatal to the thesis — the DSD moat and brand equities are real — but together they explain why the market demands proof of inflection before paying up.

7. The Snacks Portfolio: Frito-Lay, Quaker & Better-for-You

The snacks portfolio is simultaneously PepsiCo's greatest asset and its biggest question mark. Frito-Lay's DSD network — direct delivery to hundreds of thousands of retail touchpoints — is an unreplicable distribution moat that underwrites the #1 US salty-snack share. The problem is not distribution; it is demand composition.

Management's answer is portfolio modernization on three fronts: (1) affordability architecture — smaller packs and sharper price points rather than blanket cuts; (2) better-for-you expansion — the Siete Foods and Poppi acquisitions, sugar reduction, and removal of artificial ingredients, positioning for the GLP-1 and wellness shifts rather than against them; and (3) operational restructuring — the Elliott-backed program of SKU reduction, Frito-Lay plant closures, automation, and shared "mixing centers" integrating snack and beverage logistics to cut redundant infrastructure. The North America supply-chain review due late 2026 is the highest-impact catalyst in the thesis: it determines whether the cost takeout is real.

9. Competitive Position

PepsiCo competes on two fronts, and its position differs sharply between them:

  • Versus Coca-Cola: in beverages, Coca-Cola is winning decisively right now — KO's North America volume grew 3% in Q2 while PepsiCo's fell 4%, and KO's 35.6% comparable operating margin is more than double PepsiCo's 16.8%. But the companies are not the same bet: PepsiCo's snacks business has no Coca-Cola equivalent, and its DSD network gives it a route-to-market advantage in US retail that KO's bottler system does not replicate. Cam holds both deliberately — KO for beverage excellence, PEP for snacks exposure and contrarian value.
  • Versus Keurig Dr Pepper: KDP is the third US beverage player, competing on Dr Pepper, Canada Dry, and Keurig coffee. It is a credible regional competitor but lacks PepsiCo's global snacks scale and international growth engines.
  • Versus Monster and energy brands: energy is the growth pocket of beverages, and PepsiCo is contesting it through partnerships including the Alani Nu arrangement — though Q2 showed those arrangements currently pressuring PBNA margins rather than lifting them. Execution here is a 2027 story.
  • Versus private label: this is the most dangerous competitor in snacks right now. Branded salty snacks priced 47–63% above store brands are losing the value-tier shopper, and the defection has proven sticky. PepsiCo's moat is brand and DSD, not price — the strategy must be to earn the premium through innovation and pack architecture, not to chase private label down.

10. Risks

  • Value-trap risk — the central risk. If North American volumes keep sliding and the "more gradual" recovery proves to be no recovery, the 15× multiple is not cheap — it is fair for a no-growth staple, and the dividend alone cannot carry the thesis. This is the risk the BUY rating is explicitly underwriting.
  • Margin dilution from the price-for-volume trade. Q2 showed the exchange rate clearly: lower net pricing, flat volume, −40 bps of core margin. If management keeps funding share with price, margins compress further before volumes respond.
  • Guide credibility. H1 core constant-currency EPS of ~+3% against a +4–6% full-year guide requires a sharp H2 acceleration. A trim on October 8 would damage the turnaround narrative more than the numbers.
  • GLP-1 and health shifts. A structural, multi-year demand headwind for core salty snacks; the better-for-you pivot is the right response but takes years to move the mix.
  • Elliott execution risk. Activist-driven restructuring (plant closures, SKU cuts, potential bottling refranchising, non-core asset sales) can destroy as well as create value if pushed too far or too fast.
  • Input costs and FX. Commodity and logistics cost pressure plus currency translation remain swing factors on the 40-bps margin trajectory.
  • PBNA partnership drag. The Alani commercial arrangements are currently a margin headwind; if the energy bet does not scale, it is cost without benefit.

12. Catalysts & What to Watch

  • Q3 2026 earnings — October 8, 2026 (11 days out; consensus ~$2.30 EPS): the single most important near-term event. Watch NA convenient-foods net revenue (the test of whether share gains come with revenue or are bought with price), PBNA volume, and whether the 4–6% core EPS guide is reaffirmed or trimmed.
  • North America supply-chain review — due late 2026 (Elliott agreement): the highest-impact structural catalyst. Real cost takeout and plant-closure savings would underwrite 2027–28 margin expansion; a vague outcome would signal the restructuring lacks teeth.
  • Holiday season (Q4): the seasonally strongest snacking quarter — sequential volume improvement here is the minimum viable proof of stabilization.
  • Margin trajectory in H2: core operating margin must stop contracting (−40 bps in Q2) for the re-rating to begin. Watch gross margin and the PBNA −90 bps drag specifically.
  • Dividend declaration cadence: the 55th consecutive increase, expected with the early-2027 declaration cycle, would extend the King streak through the turnaround.
