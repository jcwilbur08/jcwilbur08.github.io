Investment conclusion

Walmart is the highest-quality retailer on earth, and it is quietly becoming a different business: global advertising grew 38% last quarter, membership fee revenue rose 17%, and e-commerce — now roughly a quarter of sales — has been profitable for six straight quarters with double-digit incremental margins. That mix shift is real and structural, and it justifies a premium multiple. But the market is already charging for it: about 35x forward earnings, a ~1.6% free-cash-flow yield, and a PEG near 4 leave no room for the kind of quarter Walmart just reported — U.S. comps of +2.6% missed expectations for the first time in about five years, and both Q3 and full-year EPS guidance sit below the Street. Rated Buy with a $120 twelve-month target (~15% upside; ~37x FY2028 consensus EPS of $3.22). This is a name to own for the compounding and stage on weakness — not chase at $104.

Re-rating translation (new scale, effective October 3). 1. What’s new in 24 hours: Mizuho trims, bulls hold their ground

Oct 2 – 4 · price action and news flow since the initiation

The stock closed Friday at $104.26, unchanged on the session, after a choppy week: it fell 2.7% on September 30 (to $103.92) when Mizuho cut its price target from $130 to $125 while keeping an Outperform rating, then recovered 0.3% on October 1. The 52-week range now reads $98.88–$135.16, market capitalization about $827 billion, trailing P/E ~37.6–37.8, forward P/E ~34.8, PEG ~4.0, beta 0.58.

The analyst tape since the initiation is mixed but net constructive. Mizuho’s trim is the cautious data point — a lower target on unchanged Outperform, the classic “we like it, just not the price” signal. On the bull side, DA Davidson reiterated Buy with a $132 target and KeyBanc reaffirmed Overweight with a $135 target, both citing Walmart’s expanding fashion and apparel push as a higher-margin share-gain vector. The Street consensus (MarketBeat) stands at Moderate Buy with an average target of about $131.74 across 39 firms (3 Strong Buy, 32 Buy, 4 Hold) — our $120 remains deliberately below it.

Two company items are worth logging. First, CEO John Furner’s September 25 letter (publicized October 3) formally rules out personalized pricing: Walmart will not use a customer’s income, shopping history, or urgency to set prices, will not use data shared with its Sparky AI shopping assistant to raise prices or hide cheaper options, and says digital shelf labels display approved prices rather than setting them. That closes a reputational risk before it opens. Second, Walmart launched “Shop to Light” on October 2 — an app feature that flashes an LED on the shelf label at a product’s location, doubling as a picking aid for associates on pickup and delivery orders. Small, but it is the automation-and-digital flywheel in miniature.

Housekeeping: EVP Daniel Bartlett sold 3,990 shares at $104.41 on October 1 under a pre-arranged 10b5-1 plan, retaining 618,359 shares — a 0.64% trim, immaterial. None of the 24-hour flow changes the thesis; if anything, the Mizuho trim validates the initiation’s core caution: the Street is marking down what it will pay for this growth, not the growth itself.

2. The quarter: a comps miss the market could not ignore

Q2 FY2027 — reported August 20, 2026 (quarter ended July 31)

The headline numbers were a beat: revenue of $187.9 billion rose 5.9% (+5.1% in constant currency) against roughly $186.6–186.75 billion expected, and adjusted EPS of $0.81 grew 19.1% year over year versus the $0.74 consensus. Operating income jumped 28.8% to about $9.38 billion as reported (+17.4% adjusted, constant currency), beating the roughly $8.65 billion estimate, and the gross profit rate expanded 96 basis points (Walmart U.S. up 158 bps). GAAP EPS of $0.80 compared with $0.88 a year earlier.

The market ignored all of it, because the comp missed. Walmart U.S. comparable sales excluding fuel rose 2.6% — transactions +1.5%, average ticket +1.1%, with e-commerce contributing about 510 basis points — against roughly 3.8% expected. That was the first U.S. comp miss in about five years and the slowest in about six. Sam’s Club U.S. comps ex-fuel rose 4.4% (transactions +7.0%, ticket −2.5%, e-commerce ~450 bps), and global e-commerce grew 23% (U.S. +24%, Sam’s +26%, International +19%). The stock fell roughly 9–10% on the print and sits about 17% below its April level.

Two quality flags sit inside the quarter. First, about $2.9 billion of tariff refunds boosted operating-income growth by roughly 750 basis points, according to the CFO’s commentary on the earnings call as covered in the press — this figure comes from call coverage, not the 8-K text. Excluding the net tariff impact, management said underlying operating-income growth was at the top end of its guidance, and the company is reinvesting the refunds into price rollbacks on more than 11,000 items. Second, pharmacy deflation from the Medicare Maximum Fair Price regulation (effective January 1, 2026) was a 125-basis-point headwind to U.S. comps, and management now expects roughly a 125-basis-point drag on full-FY2027 U.S. comps, up from 100. Inventory of $61.6 billion grew 6.7% (+6.0% constant currency) against 5.9% sales growth — modestly ahead, a line to watch — and Walmart U.S. expenses deleveraged 72 basis points on higher claims, depreciation, and associate healthcare costs.

Guidance was the second shoe. For Q3 FY2027 the company guided net sales growth of 3.0–3.75%, adjusted operating income growth of 2.0–4.0%, and adjusted EPS of $0.62–$0.64 against a roughly $0.68 Street expectation. Full-year guidance was raised across the board — net sales +4.0–5.0% (was +3.5–4.5%), adjusted operating income +7.0–8.5% (was +6.0–8.0%), adjusted EPS $2.80–$2.87 — but the EPS range sits below the roughly $2.90 consensus. A raised guide that still trails the Street is why the multiple, not the quarter, is the debate.

3. The mix shift: advertising, membership, and profitable e-commerce

Global ads +38% · Membership fees +17% · E-commerce ~24% of sales, profitable since Q1 FY26

This is the structural bull case, and it is working. Global advertising revenue grew 38% in Q2, with Walmart Connect up 43% excluding Vizio and International advertising up 20% led by Flipkart Ads. For context, full-FY2026 global ad revenue was about $6.4 billion, up 46%, and bullish analysts cite operating margins near 70% for the Connect business. The company closed the roughly $2.3 billion Vizio acquisition in December 2024 and added Vibe.co, a self-serve streaming-ads platform, for about $1.4 billion in August 2026 — the connected-TV inventory now plugs directly into the retail-media flywheel.

Membership income is compounding alongside it: global membership fee revenue rose 17% in Q2, with Walmart+ delivering record quarterly net additions and Sam’s Club fee revenue up 6% on member growth and Plus-tier penetration. And the e-commerce story has crossed the line skeptics waited years for — the U.S. and global e-commerce businesses turned profitable in Q1 FY2026 and have stayed there for six straight quarters, with management citing roughly double-digit incremental margins. E-commerce is about 24% of net sales; more than 36% of store-fulfilled deliveries arrive in under three hours; and the company fulfilled over 3.5 billion units same- or next-day globally. International e-commerce losses are narrowing.

The arithmetic matters more than any single line: advertising and membership are small in revenue but large in profit, and they are growing multiples faster than the core. That is what lifts the warranted multiple above a traditional retailer’s — and what has to keep compounding to justify the current one.

4. Automation, AI, and the international portfolio

~60% of U.S. stores on automated DCs · China +21.8% · PhonePe IPO targeted by March 2027

Behind the mix shift sits a decade-long automation build that is now the margin engine. About 60% of U.S. stores are serviced by automated regional distribution centers; 23 of 42 regional DCs have been retrofitted with Symbotic AI robotics; and the company targets roughly 55% of fulfillment-center volume flowing through automated facilities by the end of FY2026 at about 20% lower unit costs. Proprietary “Wallaby” large language models handle pricing and inventory decisions, and four next-generation fulfillment centers are in the pipeline. This is the structural answer to the 72-basis-point expense deleverage in the quarter — automation, not headcount, absorbs the wage and claims pressure over time.

The business is also taking share where it counts. The company reports share gains across income cohorts, with the majority of gains coming from households earning over $100,000 — the trade-down customer who arrived during inflation and stayed for the value proposition. Internationally, Q2 net sales were $35.2 billion (+7.9% constant currency) with operating income of $1.4 billion (+5.7%); China grew 21.8% in constant currency in the prior quarter with roughly half of sales digital and one-hour fulfillment, and Sam’s Club China membership income was up more than 40%. Walmex continues geographic and digital expansion.

The two India assets are the optionality. Flipkart was redomiciled to India in March 2026 ahead of a planned local IPO, now paused on market volatility. PhonePe — in which Walmart owns about 72% — revived its listing plans in September 2026: an updated draft prospectus is expected by year-end with a listing targeted by March 2027 at roughly a $10 billion valuation. PhonePe’s FY2026 revenue grew 11.5% but its loss widened 62%, so treat the IPO as a value-unlock event, not a fundamentals story.

5. Capital allocation: Dividend King economics

$0.99 annualized dividend · 53rd straight raise · $30B buyback authorization · ~1.3x leverage

Walmart is one of the rare mega-caps where the balance sheet is a genuine competitive advantage. The board raised the quarterly dividend 5.3% to $0.2475 per share ($0.99 annualized) in February 2026 — the 53rd consecutive annual increase, Dividend King status intact. At the current price the yield is only about 0.95%, but the payout ratio is roughly 36% of earnings and about 58% of free cash flow, so the dividend has decades of headroom. A new $30 billion buyback authorization was approved in February 2026; the company repurchased 42.3 million shares for $5.1 billion through the second quarter, leaving $25.1 billion remaining — a real, ongoing offset to the multiple.

Leverage is conservative for the sector: total debt of $57.2 billion against $11.5 billion of cash puts net debt/EBITDA near 1.3x. Trailing-twelve-month capex of about $29.4 billion — roughly 4% of sales — is directed at automation, supply chain, e-commerce fulfillment, and technology, the investments now showing up in the margin line. The returns confirm it: ROI of 15.4% and ROA of 8.0% in the quarter, trailing ROE near 22%, and a trailing net margin of 3.00%, up from 2.39% in FY2024 — a retailer expanding margins while growing the top line mid-single digits.

6. Valuation, target framework, and scenarios

~37.5x trailing · ~35x forward · ~1.6% FCF yield · PEG ~4

At $104.26, Walmart trades at roughly 37.5x trailing earnings and about 34.8x next-twelve-month consensus EPS of $3.00, with EV/EBITDA near 18–19x and a free-cash-flow yield around 1.6%. The dividend yields about 0.95%. Against retail peers on trailing multiples this is a full price: Target near 16x, Amazon near 21x, Costco near 48x. The premium over Target and Amazon is the market’s verdict that Walmart’s mix shift — high-margin ads, membership, profitable e-commerce — deserves a structurally higher multiple than a traditional retailer. We agree directionally; the question is magnitude, because a PEG near 4 means the multiple already assumes the transformation compounds without interruption.

$120 twelve-month target (about 15% upside). Framework: roughly 37x FY2028 consensus EPS of $3.22 — a premium multiple that credits the mix shift toward 70%-margin advertising and compounding membership income, but below the 40x-plus implied by the Street’s average target. Cross-checks: the sell-side average target is about $131.70 (roughly 26% upside; 39 firms: 3 Strong Buy, 32 Buy, 4 Hold), the median is $130, the high is $155 and the low is $110. Our target sits below the Street — we underwrite the transformation, not flawless execution at a premium price. The bear case on the Street is sharpening: one independent analysis published October 3–4 models FY2028 EPS of only $2.91 (about 9% below consensus) on diesel and rollback costs, with a $101 target — a useful reminder of where the multiple goes if the cost line, not the revenue line, drives 2027. Morningstar rates the economic moat Wide; a current Morningstar fair value estimate and star rating were not reliably visible in public sources, so they are excluded rather than guessed.

8. Risks and catalysts into year-end

The consumer is the swing factor

Risks: (1) Consumer slowdown — the rare U.S. comp miss is the warning: traffic decelerated to +1.5%, lower-income shoppers are described as stretched, and management flagged gas above $4 as a psychological headwind alongside more than $2 billion of incremental fuel costs (call commentary, not release text). (2) Tariffs — the $2.9 billion of refunds was one-time and is being reinvested into prices; imported general merchandise remains exposed, and one recent independent analysis argued FY2028 consensus leaves no room for diesel or rollback costs, modeling $2.91 against the roughly $3.22 Street. (3) Cost pressure — U.S. expenses deleveraged 72 basis points on claims, depreciation, and associate healthcare; automation is the structural offset but it is not instant. (4) Valuation — at ~35x forward with a PEG near 4, the stock has no cushion for a second consecutive soft quarter. (5) Pharmacy deflation — a roughly 125-basis-point comp drag that runs through FY2027.
