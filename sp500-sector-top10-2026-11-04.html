Market context

A relief rally with a fault line through the AI trade.

The market read the midterms as gridlock: no tax shock, no deregulation wave, and a lower chance of either party’s full programme. That was enough for the broadest rally in weeks. Underneath it, the market split the AI trade in two — the companies that buy compute went up, the companies that build and power it went down.

Why hardware fell. Three threads, all reported, none yet law. First, the projected Democratic sweep brings data-center moratorium threats in Texas, Georgia and Ohio — Texas alone is roughly 27% of U.S. data-center capacity and, with Georgia, about a third of AI data-center spending; one bank estimate puts 45% of planned capacity at risk, and roughly 40 local proposals have passed with about 77% support. Second, the memory crunch: Intel’s late-October warning, Apple’s margin guide, and handset commentary all say the same thing — memory prices are taxing every hardware margin in the chain. Third, AI-capex financing scrutiny: a $20bn debt raise at a discount by a major cloud name drew two downgrades on November 2, and one broker cut targets across the accelerator complex on November 4, Nvidia’s included ($420 to $315, Buy kept), citing financing risk rather than demand.

Valuation: the index is cheaper than its history; two sectors are not

Third-quarter blended earnings growth is running at 30.2% year on year, with 82% of reporters beating on earnings per share — but by only 1.9% on average, against a five-year average surprise of 7.5%. The beats are broad and thin. Calendar-2026 earnings growth is forecast at 32.9%. Estimates have risen faster than prices in Technology and Communication Services, which is why both screen cheap against their own history; Health Care and Staples screen expensive against theirs; Industrials trade above theirs.

Sector 01 · Information Technology · Stance: Neutral selective

The earnings are real. The ceiling is now political.

The structural driver has not changed — AI compute, networking and memory — but this week it stopped being an abstraction. The reported numbers prove the demand; the election introduced the first credible ceiling on how fast it can be built; and the memory shortage started taxing every company that consumes chips instead of selling them.

Structural drivers. AI accelerators, custom silicon, high-bandwidth memory and networking remain the fastest-growing profit pools in the index. The evidence is in the prints: AMD’s data-center revenue nearly doubled; Arista’s networking revenue rose 58.5% with a 2027 guide of roughly $18.5bn given on the call; Supermicro’s revenue nearly quadrupled, albeit on preliminary, unaudited figures at a thin 12.7% gross margin. The hyperscalers are funding it from cash flow — one raised $25bn of debt for AI this week on top of operating cash — and custom silicon deals, including a reported $63bn debt-financed chip agreement, keep Broadcom at the centre of the non-Nvidia supply chain.

Cycle position. Two cycles are running in opposite directions. Accelerators face an oversupply of orders against a shortage of power and permits. Memory is in acute shortage — the segment’s trailing revenue has more than tripled — which is wonderful for the one memory name in this ten and a margin tax on PC, handset and server makers. Intel’s warning set the tone for the consumer end: fourth-quarter revenue guided to $15–16bn against expectations near $17.1bn, PC units down 12% in 2026 with a further 10% decline planned for 2027, and no optimism offered on memory supply for 2027.

Valuation. The sector has de-rated, not cheapened to value: roughly 19–20× forward earnings against a 25.5× five-year average, because estimates rose faster than prices. Inside the ten the dispersion is the story — the two cheapest large names on verified forward multiples are Nvidia (16.54×) and Broadcom (18.70×), while Apple (36.98×) and AMD (34.77×) carry the multiple risk, and Micron’s 4.51× is a cyclical trough multiple, not a bargain signal. Sector net margin of 32.3%, against 27.5% a year ago, is the fundamental support.

What changed since October 30. AMD, Apple and Arista reported actuals; Intel warned; the November 4 session sold the hardware complex off inside a rising market; and a broker cut targets across the accelerator names (Nvidia $420→$315, Buy kept) on financing risk. Two names swapped places: AMD’s print earned rank 4; Apple’s December margin guide — 45–46%, on memory costs and AI spending — cost it rank 5 despite a record $114.5bn quarter. Qualcomm’s rank is held pending its results, which landed after the November 4 close.

  • Key risk 1

    Permitting and politics. Moratorium threats in Texas, Georgia and Ohio; an estimated 45% of planned data-center capacity at risk. Nothing is law — but build schedules, and the multiples that capitalise them, now price a political variable.

  • Key risk 2

    Memory prices. The same shortage that lifts Micron crushes PC, handset and server margins — already visible in Apple’s guide and Intel’s warning, and the live question for Qualcomm’s handset exposure.

  • Key risk 3

    AI-capex financing. Debt-funded build-outs are attracting downgrades and target cuts. If financing costs bite, the order book — not demand — becomes the constraint.

Stance: Neutral selective — unchanged

The earnings power is real, but the multiple de-rating plus a new policy ceiling argue against chasing hardware beta. Keep the stance; be selective about where new money goes — cash-generative hyperscale and software first.

Sector 02 · Health Care · Stance: Overweight

The clearest gap in the book, and the policy wind just turned.

Health Care is the only sector where the desk’s stance, the portfolio’s gap and the week’s news all point the same way. You are 6.7 points underweight the sector the desk rates highest — and the election removed more of its political tail-risk than it added.

Structural drivers. The incretin franchise (GLP-1 and successors) remains the largest new drug category in a generation, with immunology (Skyrizi/Rinvoq) as the second engine and medtech — structural heart, robotics, diagnostics — compounding behind them. The innovation pipeline is not the constraint; pricing is. U.S. list-price cuts land from January 2027, and the leading non-U.S. incretin maker’s November 4 print showed the shape of the mature phase: constant-currency sales growth of only 4%, DKK 8.7bn of impairments, and a full-year guide raised only to −2% to +2%. Note that company sits outside the S&P 500 slate ranked here, so its raise changes the thesis context, not any rank.

Cycle position. The post-legislation overhang that sat on insurers and hospitals all year is easing. On November 4 insurers and hospitals rose 3–8% on hopes for subsidy and Medicaid stability under the projected new Congress — with the important qualifier that gridlock cuts both ways: the same veto math that blocks damaging reform also blocks helpful reform, and major change is unlikely either way. That is a lower-variance policy backdrop, which is what this sector’s multiple needed.

Valuation. This is the honest cost of the call: at 20.0–20.2× forward earnings against a 17.4–17.7× five-year average, Health Care is one of only three sectors trading at a premium to its own history, and its net margin (7.5%) sits below its five-year average (8.7%). The desk tolerates the premium for two reasons: the reader’s underweight is the largest actionable gap after Technology, and the policy risk that justified a discount has fallen.

What changed since October 30. The election read-across (insurer/hospital strength), the incretin competitor’s guide raise with impairments attached, and a 3.7% November 4 rise in Amgen. No rank changes — nothing in the window altered the relative quality of these ten franchises.

  • Key risk 1

    Drug pricing and subsidy politics. Gridlock limits the downside but January 2027 list-price cuts are already scheduled; a future Congress is a different calculation.

  • Key risk 2

    GLP-1 competition and patent cliffs. The category leader outside this index grew constant-currency sales only 4% and took impairments — the category is maturing faster than the multiples assume.

  • Key risk 3

    Insurer cost trends. Medical-cost trend scrutiny on the managed-care names has not gone away; it was merely outvoted this week.

Stance: Overweight — reaffirmed

Valuation premium tolerated because the exposure gap is the clearest in the book and the policy tail-risk fell. This is where the next dollar of sector money goes.

Sector 03 · Financials · Stance: Neutral selective

The downgrade is about policy, not earnings.

Nothing went wrong with the banks this week. What went away is the reason the October edition was Overweight: a deregulation and tax-cut tailwind that required one party to hold both chambers and the White House’s agenda to move. Gridlock removes that leg — and for this portfolio, the stance was always academic anyway.

Structural drivers. Capital-markets activity, resilient credit, buybacks and the payments duopoly’s shift into value-added services remain intact. Net interest income benefits from a 10-year near 5%; the same yield level suppresses mortgage and deal volumes. The franchise quality at the top of this ten — deposit scale, network economics, underwriting discipline — is unchanged by anything that happened between October 30 and November 4.

Cycle position. Late-cycle credit, benign so far. The sector rose with the broad market on November 4, as it should have: a gridlock outcome is not a bank earnings event. The honest cycle questions are unchanged — credit normalisation, AI-linked lending exposure building inside loan books, and how long higher-for-longer flatters net interest income before it pressures borrowers.

Valuation. Fair, not cheap: 14.8–14.9× forward earnings against a 14.6× five-year average, with a 20.0% net margin essentially flat year on year. There is no valuation argument for urgency in either direction, which suits a downgrade delivered for policy reasons.

What changed since October 30. One thing only, but it was the stance’s foundation: the projected sweep means divided government, and divided government means the deregulation trade is over. The most deregulation-sensitive name in the ten — the advisory-and-trading franchise at rank 5 — loses the most from the change, though its rank, on franchise quality, is unchanged.

  • Key risk 1

    Gridlock. The upside case (deregulation, tax) is removed; what remains is the standing risk set with no offsetting policy catalyst.

  • Key risk 2

    Credit and AI-lending exposure. Late-cycle books plus fast-growing AI-linked lending are the combination to watch into 2027.

  • Key risk 3

    Rates staying higher. A 10-year pinned near 5% helps margins and hurts volumes; a move well above 5% hurts both.

Stance: Neutral selective — downgraded from Overweight selective

A policy downgrade, not an earnings downgrade. The franchises are fine; the catalyst is gone.
