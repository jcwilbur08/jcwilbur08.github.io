Desk decision — hold the position

The verdict

Buy is maintained at 7/10 conviction with the $315 twelve-month target unchanged. One print does not settle this name in either direction: the AWS demand evidence supports the rating, and the cash-flow and capital-spending evidence argues against higher conviction until the 2027 spending number is quantified.

The question going into the print was narrow and answerable: would AWS growth exceed 35%? It did, by a wide margin, and management then raised the bar itself, projecting growth above 40% in both 2027 and 2028. That is the single most important fact in the quarter. It is also why the stock’s reaction deserves care rather than dismissal. The market did not sell a cloud disappointment. It sold the cost of a cloud acceleration — $77,259 million of gross property-and-equipment purchases in one quarter, a trailing-twelve-month free cash outflow of $42,357 million, long-term debt nearly tripling during 2026, and a fourth-quarter guide whose midpoints sit below what analysts expected on both sales and operating income.

Portfolio stance

Hold the position. Do not pay up on the AWS headline, and do not add into the after-hours washout on the cash headline either. The print does not support raising the target: the $315 target was already a stretch before the print, it sits 29.2% above Thursday’s close, and nothing in the quarter improved its arithmetic. Equally, nothing in the quarter broke the demand thesis that underpins it. The next decision point is the quantification of 2027 capital spending and delivery against the fourth-quarter guide — not Friday’s tape.

The AWS verdict

Answered — decisively, and then some

AWS net sales reached $45,984 million, up 39.3% year on year (39% excluding foreign exchange), beating the single-source consensus of $45,680 million by $304 million. It was the sixth straight quarter of acceleration and the fastest growth in nearly five years. Annualised, the quarter is a $184 billion run rate. Contracted backlog reached $578 billion, more than double a year earlier, against $496 billion at June 30, 2026. Management’s projection — growth above 40% in both 2027 and 2028 — moves the debate from whether demand exists to whether Amazon can build fast enough, and at what return. On the evidence of this quarter, demand is not the constraint. Capacity is.

Three things to carry into the next quarter. First, watch the 2027 capital-spending number when it is given: “meaningfully more” than about $220 billion is a direction, not a budget, and the market will not fund an open-ended build without one. Second, watch fourth-quarter advertising: the high-teens growth guided for the quarter has to arrive after an 11% third quarter, or the softness stops looking like timing. Third, watch operating cash flow against depreciation and memory costs. AWS margins improved on the artificial-intelligence side faster than management expected, by its own account — but the cash statement, not the margin line, is where this investment cycle will be judged.

Headline miss, operating beat

The print

Amazon missed on the two lines the market watches first — sales and earnings per share — and beat where it counts operationally. Operating income of $28,076 million was the highest ever, at a 13.9% margin, and above the top of the company’s own third-quarter guidance range.

Total net sales of $201,944 million rose 12% year on year (13% excluding foreign exchange, which reduced sales by about $0.9 billion) and missed the single-source consensus of $202,250 million by $306 million, or 0.2%. GAAP diluted earnings per share of $1.89 missed consensus of $1.97 by $0.08. Net income fell to $20,685 million from $21,187 million a year earlier — but that comparison is distorted. The prior-year quarter included $9.5 billion of pre-tax gains on the Anthropic investment. The earnings miss was not an operating failure: it was driven by the swing in non-operating items, from $10,748 million of income a year ago to $1,207 million of expense this quarter, including interest expense that more than tripled to $1,691 million from $538 million.

Read the operating beat with the prior-year comparison in mind

Operating income rose 61% year on year, but the comparison flatters the result. The prior-year quarter carried roughly $2.5 billion of settlement expense and roughly $1.8 billion of severance in the comparison context, and other operating expense fell to $134 million from $2,875 million. On analyst arithmetic that strips those items out, underlying operating-income growth was closer to 29%. That is still a strong quarter — it is simply not a 61% quarter. The distinction matters because the market’s question is not whether this quarter was profitable; it is whether profitability can keep pace with a build of this size. A 29% underlying rate, against capital spending growing far faster, is the honest starting point for that debate.

Prime Day timing explains part, not all, of the retail softness. Excluding the shift in Prime Day timing, third-quarter sales growth would have been about 400 basis points higher. Online stores grew only 2%, and paid units grew 12% — the consumer was buying, but the revenue line did not show it in the quarter’s calendar. That is a timing explanation with a limit: it covers the quarter, it does not cover the advertising miss, and it will be tested directly by the fourth-quarter guide.

The 35% question, answered

AWS and AI

AWS grew 39.3% — the sixth straight quarter of acceleration, the fastest in nearly five years, and comfortably above the 35% threshold the desk set before the print. The second quarter had grown 37% to $42,232 million. The acceleration continued into a period when Amazon was lapping a year in which it had added a great deal of capacity.

The backlog is the forward statement. At $578 billion, contracted backlog more than doubled year on year and grew at a triple-digit rate, up from $496 billion at June 30, 2026, and described by management as well diversified. Backlog of that size, growing that fast, is what turns a strong quarter into a multi-year claim — and it is what management leaned on when projecting growth above 40% in both 2027 and 2028, and when describing a business that could become a trillion-dollar revenue business within five to ten years, alongside the stores business.

Capacity, not demand

The constraint is the build

Before the print, on October 23, the chief executive said AWS capacity was sold out for 2026, largely sold out for 2027, with a substantial portion of 2028 committed. On the earnings call he returned to the same point: Amazon is installing an artificial-intelligence accelerator about every 14 seconds, and customers are taking up capacity almost as fast as it is installed. Core compute and storage revenue grew at their fastest rates in several years. Management’s position is that even the planned 2026 build — about $220 billion of cash capital expenditure — would still not have been enough capacity to meet all the demand it sees, and that the same dynamic would hold in 2027. An investor should read that as both reassurance and warning: demand is verified, but the company is now in a race between construction, component supply — memory costs are explicitly called out — and depreciation.

What the release shows on AI, and what it does not

The release records concrete AI traction without attaching a revenue figure to it. Bedrock token volume rose about 17 times year on year, and spending by Fortune 500 companies on Bedrock rose about seven times. AgentCore serves tens of thousands of customers, with twice as many agents deployed in the third quarter as in the prior three quarters combined. Lambda passed a $1 billion annualised run rate. Bedrock added new model families, and TorchNeuron was presented as letting existing PyTorch code run on Trainium without rewriting. What the release and the call reporting do not establish is a third-quarter AI revenue run rate. The figure of more than $25 billion, growing at triple digits, belongs to the second quarter and must not be carried forward as a third-quarter number. Likewise, no third-quarter call statement on Trainium revenue or on Anthropic specifically was verified from a transcript source read for this report, and none is asserted here. The Anthropic context that is verified is pre-print: Anthropic’s filing showed at least $110 billion of Amazon Web Services spend commitments into the 2030s, and Amazon’s Anthropic stake was valued at about $190 billion at June 30, 2026.

On margins, management’s claim is striking and should be held to evidence over the next two quarters: operating margins on the artificial-intelligence side are improving rapidly, well ahead of where they were on the core offering at a similar stage of evolution, even with heightened component costs such as memory and significant depreciation from the capital expenditure being invested. AWS operating margin was 38.7% in the quarter, up from 34.6% a year ago but down from 39.4% in the second quarter. The direction over a year is favourable; the direction over a quarter is a reminder that the build carries costs before it carries revenue.

Advertising: the soft spot, with a timing explanation

Advertising services revenue of $19,637 million grew 11% (12% excluding foreign exchange), down from 26% in the second quarter, and missed the single-source consensus of $20,910 million by $1,273 million. Amazon attributed the slowdown to Prime Day timing and a couple of other timing factors, and guided fourth-quarter advertising growth to the high teens. That guidance is now a testable claim: high-teens growth in the fourth quarter, against an 11% third quarter, requires the timing explanation to be right and complete. If it is, the miss is a calendar artefact. If fourth-quarter advertising lands in the low teens again, the explanation fails and the advertising franchise — one of the highest-margin revenue streams in the company — needs a different account.

Prime Video, reported on the call, has a revenue run rate in the tens of billions, is profitable in its own right, and its advertising tier reaches more than 315 million monthly active users globally, more than 130 million in the United States. The release also records the launch of an advertising agent and full-funnel campaign tools. None of that offsets a $1.27 billion consensus miss in the quarter; it frames why the fourth-quarter advertising guide matters more than usual.

Cash: the market’s problem, stated plainly

Operating cash flow over the trailing twelve months was $168,300 million, up 29% from $130,691 million. Free cash flow over the same period was an outflow of $42,357 million, against an inflow of $14,788 million a year earlier — a swing of about $57 billion. In the quarter itself, gross purchases of property and equipment were $77,259 million; net of $1,381 million of proceeds, net purchases were $75,878 million. Trailing-twelve-month net purchases were $210,657 million, up 82% year on year. The two figures — $77.3 billion gross, $75.9 billion net — are both used in secondary reporting; they are not the same number, and this report keeps them distinct.

The balance sheet funded the gap with debt. Long-term debt stood at $173,125 million at September 30, 2026, against $65,648 million at December 31, 2025. Interest expense in the quarter tripled year on year. The 2026 cash capital-expenditure expectation was held at about $220 billion, and management said spending would be “meaningfully more” in 2027, with no numeric figure verified. The inference — labelled as such — is that trailing free cash flow stays deeply negative through the build unless AWS monetisation outruns depreciation and memory costs. That is not a forecast in this report; it is the arithmetic consequence of the numbers management has given, and it is the reason conviction was not raised.

The consumer, on the release’s evidence

No direct third-quarter call statement on consumer health was verified, so this report does not attribute one. The release’s operating evidence is: paid units up 12%; grocery, fresh and essentials growing about three times faster than the rest of the store and accounting for about half of units; more than 1.5 billion items delivered same-day or overnight, the fastest third-quarter speeds ever; and the rapid-delivery format now in more than 450 towns and cities, with items ordered ten times higher year on year. Selection from notable brands rose more than 20% year on year. The consumer in this quarter bought more units, faster — the revenue softness was a calendar and mix story, not a units story.

Headcount and newer businesses

Employees numbered 1,638,000, up 4% year on year from 1,578,000, excluding contractors and temporary staff. The release records plans to hire more than 300,000 seasonal, part-time and full-time operations employees in the United States for the holiday season, alongside more than $1.5 billion of investment raising the minimum starting pay for full-time United States operations employees to $20 per hour, plus a grocery discount and a lifetime banking benefit. No third-quarter call statement on headcount or efficiency was verified. Beyond the headline segments, the release records paid robotaxi rides in Las Vegas with testing in San Diego and Houston and operations in 12 United States cities; pharmacy three-hour delivery in more than 350 cities with customers up 60% year on year; and shopping-assistant interactions up 570% year on year. The satellite service, now named Amazon Leo, remains on track to begin initial service later this year, with a strategic agreement under which a major United States telecom provider will be the first to offer Leo connectivity to enterprise and public-sector customers. No third-quarter call statement on the satellite service, launch count, or cost capitalisation was verified.

A straddle, not a raise

Guidance

Fourth-quarter guidance straddled expectations: the ranges reach above consensus at the top and below it at the bottom, and both midpoints sit below what analysts expected. That is a guide that neither confirms the AWS acceleration nor dispels the cost concern — it leaves both open into the holiday quarter.

The sales guide anticipates about 60 basis points of unfavourable foreign-exchange impact. The guidance assumes no impact from energy-derivative remeasurements and no additional acquisitions, restructurings, or legal settlements. No full-year sales or earnings-per-share guidance is given, consistent with Amazon’s practice, and no numeric full-year commentary beyond the capital-expenditure expectation was given. Why the straddle matters: a guide whose midpoint misses on both lines, after a quarter in which operating income beat the company’s own guide, reads as management choosing not to chase the AWS headline with a fourth-quarter promise. That is prudent if the constraint is capacity — a company that cannot build fast enough should not promise revenue it cannot serve. It is also the reason the after-hours reaction, which took the stock well below the regular-session close, cannot be dismissed as an overreaction to a single missed line. The guide told the market the holiday quarter will be good, not exceptional, while the spending to serve 2027 and 2028 accelerates. The desk’s view is that the guide is honest rather than alarming — but honesty about a straddle is still a straddle, and the target is not raised on it.

In management’s words

The earnings call

No full transcript of the third-quarter call was located by Thursday evening Eastern time, and no direct third-quarter remark from the chief financial officer was verified. What follows is drawn from the primary release and from attributed reporting of the call, with the attribution stated in the text. Where a statement comes from before the print, it is labelled as pre-print.

“In AWS, our plan is working. Despite lapping a comparable year-over-year period where we added a lot of capacity, AWS grew 39.3% year over year, accelerating for the sixth straight quarter, our fastest growth in nearly five years. Backlog is $578 billion, growing triple digits year over year and well diversified. We are installing an AI accelerator about every 14 seconds, and almost as fast as we install capacity, customers are gobbling it up. Our core compute and storage revenue grew at their fastest rates in several years. Our operating margins have long been strong on our core offering, but rapidly improving on the AI side too, well ahead of where they were on core at a similar time of evolution, even with heightened component costs like memory, and significant depreciation from the CapEx we’re investing. We continue to accelerate our capex payback and have even more conviction than we already had that the investment we’re making in AWS will yield a much larger revenue and free cash flow generating business than we would have imagined a couple years ago with strong return on invested capital.” — Andy Jassy, chief executive, on the Q3 2026 earnings call, as reported in call coverage

Three sentences in that passage carry the investment case, and each deserves a separate check. “Accelerating for the sixth straight quarter” is verified by the reported growth rate and the prior-quarter comparison. “Backlog is $578 billion” is the release’s forward claim and the largest single number in the quarter after the spending figures. “We continue to accelerate our capex payback” is the claim the cash statement has not yet shown: payback accelerating is a statement about the future shape of returns, made in a quarter in which free cash flow was deeply negative. The desk takes the first two as established and the third as a promise to be verified against the 2027 spending number and the delivery of the backlog.

“There has never been a trillion dollar revenue business, and we may have two of them in the next 5–10 years.” — Andy Jassy, in the primary earnings release, clarified on the call as referring to AWS and the stores business

On the call, the projection behind that sentence was put numerically: AWS revenue growth above 40% in both 2027 and 2028, and a business that could be a trillion-dollar revenue business in five to ten years. A projection of that kind, from a chief executive on an earnings call, is a commitment device: it gives the market a number to hold management to, and it raises the cost of the capacity build failing to convert. It is also, on its face, extraordinary — sustained growth above 40% on a $184 billion run rate implies AWS revenue roughly doubling in under two years. The backlog supports the direction; the capacity constraint is the reason the number is a projection rather than a certainty.

Capacity, in the chief executive’s own framing

Before the print, speaking on October 23, Jassy said AWS capacity was sold out for 2026, largely sold out for 2027, with a substantial portion of 2028 committed. That pre-print framing is the context for the call’s accelerator remark and for the capital-spending guide. The company’s position, stated before the print and repeated in substance on the call, is that even at about $220 billion of 2026 spending it would still not have enough capacity to meet all the demand it sees in 2026, and that the dynamic would also hold in 2027. Read together, the statements describe a company choosing to overbuild rather than under-serve — and asking shareholders to fund the choice before the returns arrive.

Risks, in order of weight

  • The build outruns the return. Capital spending of about $220 billion in 2026, “meaningfully more” in 2027, against a $42,357 million trailing free-cash outflow and debt nearly tripling in nine months. If AWS monetisation, depreciation, and memory costs move against the company, the backlog converts slowly and the balance sheet carries the interim.
  • Capacity does not convert. Sold-out capacity is only valuable if it is installed on time, at the guided cost, and filled with paying workloads. Component costs, power, and construction timelines are the practical constraints behind the “every 14 seconds” claim.
  • Advertising softness is not timing. If fourth-quarter advertising growth does not reach the high teens, a high-margin franchise is decelerating for reasons beyond Prime Day, and the retail profit engine weakens just as the cloud build peaks.
  • The guide is the ceiling, not the floor. Both fourth-quarter midpoints sit below expectations. A holiday quarter that lands at the bottom of the guided ranges would put the $315 target’s arithmetic under strain well before any 2027 revision.
  • Anthropic distortion cuts both ways. Trailing earnings flattered by investment gains make trailing multiples unreliable, and the absence of those gains in the current quarter is part of why earnings per share missed. Any valuation that leans on trailing earnings without adjusting for them will misstate both cheapness and expensiveness.
  • Concentration of the thesis. The entire case now rests on one segment growing above 40% for two years. A single-segment thesis with a balance-sheet-funded build is a higher-conviction claim than a diversified one, and the 7/10 rating reflects that concentration rather than hiding it.

What would change our mind

To raise conviction or the target: a quantified 2027 capital-spending figure that the backlog and cash generation can plausibly fund; fourth-quarter advertising growth in the high teens as guided; and a post-print street revision that engages with the AWS projection rather than merely repricing the cash outflow. To cut conviction or the target: a 2027 spending figure without a funding path; fourth-quarter advertising growth in the low teens; a fourth-quarter operating-income outcome at the bottom of the guided range; or evidence that capacity installation is slipping against the sold-out demand. Falsification is specific: if AWS growth decelerates from 39.3% while spending accelerates, the capacity-constraint account fails and the build becomes the thesis’s liability rather than its evidence. Until one of those conditions is met, Buy at 7/10 and $315 stands — maintained, not endorsed more strongly, and not abandoned on one evening’s tape.
