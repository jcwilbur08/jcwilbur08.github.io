01 · The verdict

Downgraded to Buy, 6/10, $240. The cut is a multiple de-rating, not an earnings cut.

NVIDIA is downgraded from Strong Buy, 8/10, $328 (October 14) to Buy, conviction 6/10, with a $240 twelve-month target. The business case stays strong. The thesis’s central pillar — Jensen Huang’s founder-operator leadership — is gone, with no successor and no timeline, and the print that was supposed to settle the year now arrives under an interim CEO with an unresolved investigation, a congressional hearing, and three DOJ inquiries hanging over it.

The old $328 target implied 20.6 times fiscal 2028 consensus earnings of $15.91. The new $240 target is 15 times that same earnings figure — $238.65, rounded to $240. No earnings forecast was cut to get there: the desk is not underwriting lower earnings, it is underwriting a lower multiple for the same earnings, because a company whose founder ran it for 33 years, with no succession plan and no visible number two, cannot command the multiple it commanded under him until the market knows who runs it next and what the investigations conclude.

That is the same move the Street made in two days — Bernstein and Stifel explicitly held earnings forecasts broadly intact and halved the multiple — and the desk is making it for the same reason, just not as far. Bernstein’s new $200 is about 12.7 times fiscal 2028 earnings; Stifel’s $205 is about 13 times; Evercore’s $186 is 8 times its own, higher 2028 earnings estimate. At $166.19 the stock already trades at 10.4 times fiscal 2028 consensus earnings, below every one of those de-rated targets except Evercore’s. The desk’s $240 sits between the Street’s de-rated centre and the old $328, and it still carries 44.4% upside from the close.

Conviction is 6/10, not higher, because three things are unknowable tonight: who the permanent CEO is, whether the November 17 print and call under interim CEO Colette Kress confirm guidance, and whether the investigation and government inquiries produce anything that touches the business. Conviction is not lower, because nothing in the reported numbers has changed: data-centre revenue grew 117% in the last reported quarter, hyperscaler capital spending rose 84% year over year in the third calendar quarter, Rubin is in full production, and the balance sheet carries $23.22 billion of net cash with roughly $235 billion of buyback authorisation behind it.

What this report does not do

It does not settle the allegations. The espionage claim, the $7.5 billion chip-diversion claim, and the second-affair report are all allegations, not findings — none has been independently verified, no charge has been filed against NVIDIA or Huang, and the desk treats them as an overhang on the multiple, not as a charge against the business. Every unverified or single-source claim in this report is flagged as such in the appendix, and none of them is used to underwrite an earnings cut.

02 · What happened

The board accepted Huang’s resignation on November 11. The filing gives no reason.

NVIDIA’s Form 8-K, filed November 12, says the board accepted the resignation of Jen-Hsun Huang as president, chief executive officer, and member of the board, effective immediately. It names an interim chief executive and a first-ever board chair. It says nothing else about why.

What the filing and the accompanying press release do not contain was verified by reading the full 126-line filing: no reason for the resignation; no severance, separation, equity, or advisory arrangement for Huang; no mention of Alice Huang, Kimberly Powell, Megaspeed, the independent investigation, Congress, or any government inquiry; no mention of Huang’s shareholding or any future role. Huang is not quoted in the release.

The interim leadership

Colette M. Kress, 59, executive vice president and chief financial officer since September 2013, is interim chief executive officer and keeps the CFO job while the board searches for a permanent chief executive. Before NVIDIA she spent about eight years in finance roles at Texas Instruments, thirteen years at Microsoft (1997–2010, including CFO of the Server & Tools division from 2006), and 2010–2013 as senior vice president and CFO of Cisco’s Business Technology and Operations Finance organisation. She fronts every earnings call alongside Huang and authored the quarterly CFO commentary — she is the market-facing continuity point. In the release she said NVIDIA’s strength “has always come from its people” and that she is “committed to building on the extraordinary foundation Jensen created over the last 33 years.” No revised compensation package for the interim role had been disclosed as of November 12.

Harvey C. Jones, 73, a director since 1993 — the company’s founding year — is the board’s first-ever chair. NVIDIA had never had a separate chair before. Jones was chief executive of Synopsys from 1987 to 1998 (taking it public in 1992) and later its executive chairman; he founded Daisy Systems in 1981 and Tensilica in 1997, and has been managing partner of Square Wave Ventures, a private family office, since 2004. In the release he said the board “has full confidence in Colette and the leadership team, and we expect the company’s operations, customer commitments and product roadmap to continue without interruption.”

Huang’s remaining stake

Huang resigned from every role — president, chief executive, and director — and no advisory, chairman-emeritus, or consultancy role is disclosed. He retains his shareholding: about 3.6% of the company, approximately 870.6 million shares as of the March 23 proxy, worth more than $100 billion at recent prices. No filing found as of the evening of November 12 discloses any post-resignation sale, lock-up, or voting arrangement for that stake.

The succession search

The board “is initiating a search for a permanent chief executive and will consider internal and external candidates.” No timeline was given, no search firm was named, and no candidate is named in any company document. That is the whole official position — and it is the central problem for the rating, because no succession plan existed publicly. Huang “has never anointed a successor,” and had said he does not believe in succession planning. Evercore’s Mark Lipacis put it plainly on November 12: NVIDIA has “no obvious succession plan, nor a visible #2 operational or strategic leader,” and an extended search could leave it “in a leadership limbo.”

Names mentioned in press speculation — not company-endorsed, all speculative — include, internally, Kress herself, Ian Buck (vice president of hyperscale and high-performance computing, and the creator of CUDA), chief technology officer Michael Kagan, 32-year veteran Jeff Fisher, and Huang’s two children, both NVIDIA executives; and externally, Arm chief executive Rene Haas, AMD chief executive Lisa Su, Apple’s former chief executive Tim Cook (proposed publicly by President Trump on Truth Social on November 12, with no indication Cook wants the job or the board has considered him), Apple hardware chief Johny Srouji, former Intel chief executive Pat Gelsinger, Micron chief executive Sanjay Mehrotra, Marvell chief executive Matt Murphy, and AWS chief executive Matt Garman. Reporting also noted that Buck “lacks the operational experience typically expected of a CEO.” The desk assigns no probability to any name; the point is the breadth of the speculation, which is itself a measure of how little is known.

Two items to keep separate from the filing

Employee meeting: Kress’s first message to employees acknowledged “an extraordinarily difficult period” and announced a company-wide meeting on November 18 at 9:00am PT. This is single-source, relayed via The Verge — reported, not company-confirmed in a filing. Customers and partners: no public statement from Microsoft, Amazon, Google, Meta, CoreWeave, Oracle, or any major customer or partner in response to the resignation was found as of the evening of November 12. Do not infer endorsement or concern from silence.

03 · The overhang

Three investigations, a hearing, and a set of allegations nobody has verified.

The resignation did not close any of the open matters. It left them without a named chief executive to answer for them, and in one case — the board’s own independent investigation — without any confirmation that it is still running.

The independent investigation: status unconfirmed

On November 8 the board said a committee of independent directors, assisted by outside independent counsel, would oversee a “thorough and independent investigation” into reports that Huang was the target of a foreign intelligence effort, and that NVIDIA had “no reason to believe that either national security or NVIDIA secrets were compromised.” On November 10 the independent directors confirmed the counsel: Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz, led by Joon H. Kim, former acting US Attorney for the Southern District of New York. The scope, as described by governance experts, ran to three lines: whether law was broken, whether Huang violated internal policies, and whether the board was misled — how forthcoming he was, and when he was on notice.

The November 12 8-K and press release do not mention the investigation at all. No company statement on November 12 was found saying it continues, is complete, or will publish findings, and Reuters had already noted on November 11 that NVIDIA gave no timetable and did not say whether findings would be made public. The only source found asserting continuation is USA Today’s November 12 wording that “the Board continues to investigate” — press characterisation, not a company disclosure. The desk flags it as unverified. A shareholder class action was filed on November 9 — Kistenmacher v. NVIDIA Corp., in the Northern District of California, proposed class period October 9, 2025 to November 8, 2026, alleging misleading statements about Megaspeed dealings — with a lead-plaintiff deadline of January 8, 2027. Those are allegations only; no class has been certified and no finding has been made.

Congress: he agreed to testify; no subpoena was issued

The original request came on November 4 (letter made public November 5) from three House Republican chairs — Brian Mast (Foreign Affairs), John Moolenaar (Select Committee on the CCP/China), and Rick Crawford (Intelligence) — asking Huang, as chief executive, for a joint closed-door briefing on November 18 on his interactions with Alice Huang and NVIDIA’s safeguards against foreign espionage, with a response requested by November 11. It was an invitation, not a subpoena. Moolenaar told Fox Business on November 11 that Huang had “until the end of the day” to respond; Mast told ABC News he was prepared to subpoena him if he failed to respond.

On November 12 it was reported that Huang agreed on Wednesday, November 11, to testify — hours before NVIDIA announced his resignation — after the subpoena threats. Mast said the hearing will take place before Thanksgiving, behind closed doors, filmed and transcribed, with the Intelligence and China committees joining Foreign Affairs. The original November 18 date is therefore superseded as a standalone date in that reporting. No subpoena had been issued in any source found as of the evening of November 12. Whether Huang will now appear as former chief executive, and whether Kress or another NVIDIA representative is also expected, was not stated. The briefing was requested of Huang personally — about his relationship — not of NVIDIA as a company, so his resignation does not on its face extinguish the request, and Mast’s statement treats it as proceeding.

DOJ, FBI, and Commerce

On November 12, Semafor and Bloomberg reported three DOJ inquiries: an antitrust investigation into NVIDIA’s business practices; a Megaspeed chip-smuggling investigation, which DOJ is said to be taking the lead on; and a Manhattan US Attorney inquiry into whether entities linked to Alice Huang were involved in smuggling Super Micro Computer products to China. A separate DOJ espionage investigation is examining Alice Huang’s relationship with Jensen Huang. The key qualifier, repeated across sources: there is no indication prosecutors intend criminal charges against NVIDIA or Jensen Huang, and Bloomberg said Huang is “not a target” of US law-enforcement investigations. Trump told reporters the FBI is investigating; no FBI statement, scope, or target was found.

The history matters here. The Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security began expressing concerns in 2024 and investigated whether Megaspeed illegally brought embargoed processors into China; that investigation “yielded little success” and in 2025 was forwarded to the FBI after a letter from NVIDIA. DOJ asked BIS to pause its work in late 2025. Senator Michael Bennet wrote on November 11 to four senior administration officials seeking an update on the Megaspeed investigation by November 24. In March 2026 prosecutors charged Super Micro co-founder Yih-Shyan “Wally” Liaw over an alleged scheme to funnel NVIDIA-powered servers to China via a Bangkok company; Super Micro itself was not charged and is cooperating. Separately, Singapore’s Ministry of Home Affairs said on November 12 that Alice Huang was barred from re-entering Singapore after police determined she knowingly gave inaccurate information to downplay her role in Megaspeed; police concluded there was insufficient evidence that she had committed offences relating to the inaccurate declarations, and the investigation into Megaspeed and six other individuals is ongoing.

Alleged, not established — read this box before trading on any of it

The second affair: the New York Times reported on November 11 that Huang had an extramarital relationship with Kimberly Powell, 48, NVIDIA’s vice president of healthcare, one of roughly 60 executives reporting directly to him, citing five people who had worked at NVIDIA, speaking anonymously. Two sources said video footage showed Huang and Powell being affectionate at a hotel in San Jose during the GTC conference in March 2026; the Times said it had not independently viewed the footage. Neither Powell nor Huang has publicly confirmed the reported relationship; NVIDIA declined to comment. No link to espionage is alleged for Powell. Status: allegation from a single newspaper’s anonymous sources, unconfirmed.

The $7.5 billion chip claim: The Information reported on November 10, citing two acquaintances of Alice Huang, that she, her husband, and her sister built a network that allegedly moved about 240,000 H100/H200 and some A100 chips, worth at least $7.5 billion, into China since 2023. Reuters explicitly said it could not independently verify the report. Megaspeed denies wrongdoing; NVIDIA says it visited Megaspeed sites and found no evidence of diversion. Status: single-outlet claim, unverified, denied by the parties.

The $4.8 billion figure is different: the New York Times reported on November 9 that Megaspeed’s Malaysian affiliate Speedmatrix bought at least $4.8 billion of NVIDIA-powered hardware between June 2024 and February 2026, from trade records. That is a purchases figure, not a finding of diversion to China. Do not conflate the two numbers.

The espionage allegation: US intelligence officials concluded Alice Huang was passing information to Beijing, as reported by the New York Times on October 29; Reuters could not independently verify the intelligence allegation, which China has denied. No charge has been filed against Alice Huang, Megaspeed, or Jensen Huang, and no public finding that secrets were compromised exists. A November 12 CNBC report on ownership links between Alice Huang and a data centre serving Huawei itself notes that the ownership links alone do not demonstrate an unauthorised transfer, and the investigation uncovered no evidence of unauthorised NVIDIA chip transfers.

Rubin, margins, and the supply constraint

Rubin (Vera Rubin) is now in full production, with production shipments commencing in August 2026 and racks running at CoreWeave, Google Cloud, Microsoft Azure, Oracle Cloud Infrastructure, and Nebius. Management expects Rubin to be about 20% of Data Center revenue in the third quarter — roughly $20 billion of roughly $100 billion — and calls it the fastest ramp in company history. Revenue opportunity per gigawatt, per Huang on the call: Hopper about $18 billion, Grace Blackwell about $25 billion, Vera Rubin about $40 billion.

The margin path is the number to listen for on the call: gross margin 75.0% in the second quarter, 74% guided for the third quarter, troughing at 71–72% in the fourth quarter as memory costs soar, then 72–73% in fiscal 2028. Each point of gross margin at $108 billion of revenue is about $1.1 billion of quarterly gross profit (desk arithmetic). The fourth-quarter trough is a memory-cost story, not a demand story — and the call’s tone on it will matter more than the third-quarter print itself.

For fiscal 2028, management gave a preliminary outlook on the August call — the first time it had given a full-year-ahead forecast — of revenue growth of about 70%, which Huang called supply-constrained: customer forecasts pointed to demand roughly doubling next year, but NVIDIA expects about 70% because it cannot build fast enough. Supply and capacity commitments stood at $279 billion at July 26, up from $119 billion the prior quarter, largely memory procurement for the Rubin ramp. The company describes itself as supply-limited, not demand-limited.

Pre-print drift: what was and wasn’t found

No firm-by-firm third-quarter estimate revision table for November 10–12 was found, and no November 10–12 channel-check note was found — flagged as a gap. What was found: Zacks’ consensus EPS revised up 0.61% over 30 days to $2.49, with the most recent revisers slightly bearish (Most Accurate Estimate below consensus); MarketBeat’s bar ($2.47 / $108.80bn) unchanged from its November 2 print; and Stifel expecting NVIDIA “to deliver a modest earnings beat on November 17” even while cutting its target. The dominant pre-print revision since November 10 is not to third-quarter estimates but to valuation multiples and ratings.

No hyperscaler has guided a 2027 capital-spending cut in the sources found.

Competition: Broadcom and Anthropic

Broadcom said Anthropic is “on track to become our largest XPU customer in 2027 and sustain that in 2028,” and guides AI semiconductor revenue of about $58 billion in fiscal 2026, about $115 billion in fiscal 2027, and about $230 billion in fiscal 2028 — doubling each year. The Anthropic capacity is about 3.5 gigawatts of Google TPU capacity from 2027, on top of more than 1 gigawatt in 2026, with Anthropic to add about 15 gigawatts of TPU capacity in 2027–2028. This is a competitive threat — custom silicon and TPUs displacing NVIDIA at a frontier lab — and a financing-structure warning, not evidence that hyperscalers have cut NVIDIA orders. No customer has publicly said it is cutting NVIDIA orders since November 11.

China: licensed, small, and assumed at zero

Guidance assumes no Data Center compute revenue from China, and Hopper data-centre shipments to China were under 1% of Data Center revenue in the second quarter. H200 sales to approved Chinese customers were allowed under a case-by-case licensing regime effective January 15, 2026, with the US to receive 25% of proceeds — implemented as a 25% tariff on chips brought into the US for testing, which NVIDIA has said it cannot pass on to customers. In practice very little has shipped: the BIS Under Secretary told a House hearing in July 2026 that only a “very small quantity” had shipped, and NVIDIA took a $0.4 billion H200 charge in the first half of fiscal 2027. On November 7, Commerce approved about 24 Chinese companies to buy or handle H200s, including Baidu, BYD, and Tesla Shanghai — but two approved firms, New Matrix and Maginfra, had apparently vacant registered offices, and no revocation had been published as of November 11. The US–China trade truce runs to January 10, 2027. Commerce is also finalising rules that would restrict Chinese AI developers from renting high-end NVIDIA processors hosted outside mainland China.

One open item: the compute-financing platforms announced in August — partnerships with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs, and KKR to mobilise over $500 billion of third-party capital for AI-infrastructure buildout over time, subject to definitive agreements. The $500 billion is a design target for third-party capital, not NVIDIA revenue, and NVIDIA may provide residual-value support of up to 25% of an opportunity, assessed project by project. No definitive-agreement update was found as of November 12 — flagged as open.

Target method, stated plainly

$240 = 15 × fiscal 2028 consensus EPS of $15.91 = $238.65, rounded to $240. Fifteen times is below the Street’s de-rated centre of roughly 12.7–13 times only in the sense that it is above it — the desk is paying for the earnings power the Street is still underwriting, while refusing to pay the 20.6 times the old target implied. Sensitivity (desk arithmetic): at 15 times, a 5% cut to fiscal 2028 EPS ($15.91 → $15.11) would imply $226.72, down 5.0% on the price holding the multiple constant. Any larger price fall on an earnings cut would require the multiple to compress as well — which is the mechanism Evercore and others are arguing, not a property of the arithmetic. On fiscal 2027 EPS of $9.31, the same exercise gives 15× = $139.65, 18× = $167.58, 20× = $186.20, and 35.2× (the old target’s fiscal 2027 multiple) = $327.71.
