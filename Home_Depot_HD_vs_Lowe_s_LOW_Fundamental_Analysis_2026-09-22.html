§0 — The one-paragraph version, and a note on method

Both stocks are telling the same story from different starting points: a housing market frozen by 7% mortgage rates, a DIY consumer trading down while Pro spend holds up, and management teams responding by buying their way into higher-scale, lower-margin professional distribution — HD via SRS/GMS ($23B+ since 2024), LOW via ADG/FBM ($10B since mid-2025). Both paused buybacks to fund it, both are carrying more leverage than they want, and both companies' most recent "beat" was flattered by a one-time IEEPA tariff refund that turns a reported EPS gain into a real earnings decline once removed — HD's headline +5.1% Q2 adjusted-EPS growth is -6.0% clean; LOW's +1.6% is -0.9% clean. Neither stock is a screaming buy at today's price: on probability-weighted scenarios, HD offers roughly a 9.2%/yr five-year expected return at $305 and LOW roughly 9.8%/yr at $196 — both modestly short of a clean 10% hurdle. The call: Lowe's is the better price today; Home Depot is the better business. Start with a staged LOW position now, and build HD on any pullback toward the high-$280s/low-$290s rather than chasing it here.

This report runs the same spine used for every name in this book — strip-the-noise, five-year financials, moat assessment, bear/base/bull scenarios with 5-year IRRs, and a reverse-DCF "what's priced in" check — in parallel for both companies, then puts them head-to-head so the deployment-order question has a quantitative answer, not just a qualitative one. Fiscal years below use each company's own convention (HD's fiscal year ends ~Feb 1; Lowe's ends ~Jan 31, one year behind the calendar it's mostly in).

1. History, business model, and the same playbook

Two duopolists, one M&A strategy

Home Depot and Lowe's have run the U.S. home-improvement retail duopoly since the 1990s, together commanding the large-format hardware-and-building-materials category with no third national competitor of scale. Both are now executing the identical strategic pivot at the same moment: acquire specialty Pro-trade distribution to capture the ~55% of category spend that a DIY-focused big-box store structurally cannot reach — large contractor jobs, planned trade spend, and materials that never touch a retail shelf. HD moved first, closing SRS Distribution (roofing/landscaping/pool, $18.25B) in June 2024 and GMS (wallboard/steel framing, ~$5.5B) in late 2025, plus the smaller Mingledorff's HVAC distributor (~$1.1B) via SRS in May 2026. Lowe's followed roughly a year behind, closing Artisan Design Group (~$1.3B) in mid-2025 and Foundation Building Materials (~$8.8B) in October 2025 — its largest acquisition in 104 years. Both deals were debt-funded; both companies paused share buybacks as a direct result.

Business model

The core retail economics are unchanged: buy building materials and home-improvement products at scale, mark them up, and monetize a footprint of 1,700-2,400+ stores plus a growing Pro/installation/rental services layer. The acquired distribution businesses run a structurally different model — lower gross margin, higher inventory turns, credit-account relationships with contractors — that dilutes blended company margin even as it adds revenue and deepens the moat against a would-be third entrant. Both managements describe the math the same way: trade a few points of margin for a much larger addressable Pro wallet and stickier, less cyclical revenue.

2. Moat and competitive landscape

Where the moat is real — for both

  • Scale duopoly with no credible third entrant. No competitor has tried to build a third national big-box home-improvement chain in twenty-five years; the real-estate footprint, supplier relationships and Pro-credit infrastructure are not easily replicated.
  • The Pro-distribution layer raises switching costs. A contractor who finances materials through SRS's or FBM's trade-credit platform, or who gets same-day jobsite delivery, has a reason to stay that a price-comparison DIY shopper does not.
  • Real estate and logistics built over decades. Neither company's store network, distribution-center footprint or supplier terms can be replicated by a new entrant inside a normal investment horizon.

Where the moat is thinner than the bulls admit

  • The Pro-distribution "moat" is being bought, not built — and the market already prices a version of it without the acquisition debt. Ferguson Enterprises (FERG), a pure-play plumbing/HVAC/PVF distributor that built its Pro platform organically rather than bolting on acquired distributors, trades at 19.3x trailing / ~19.4x forward earnings with net debt/equity of ~1.0x — a cleaner balance sheet than either HD or LOW carries today, at a similar multiple to HD's and a premium to LOW's. That HD and LOW are paying up to acquire capability the market already values organically, rather than earning a scarcity premium for it, is a genuine tell.
  • Neither moat has stopped margin compression. HD's operating margin has fallen 260bp since FY22 (15.3%→12.7%); LOW's is down from a FY23 peak of 13.4% to 11.8%. The Pro push is adding revenue faster than it's adding profit.
  • Both remain fully exposed to the same macro switch: mortgage rates. Housing turnover — the single biggest driver of large-ticket renovation spend — has not turned in either company's numbers. Both managements say explicitly they are not counting on a housing recovery in guidance; if one arrives, it lifts both stocks together, and if it doesn't, neither escapes.

3. Growth drivers

Driver 1 — Pro-distribution consolidation (proven, compounding, but margin-dilutive)

This is the whole current growth story for both. HD's SRS+GMS+Mingledorff's platform is roughly two years further along than LOW's ADG+FBM platform (closed October 2025), which matters: HD has already absorbed one round of integration-cost drag and is lapping easier comparisons, while LOW is still inside the messiest year of combining FBM's ~$6.5B of revenue and ~$635M of pro forma EBITDA into its own systems. Bellinger (Mizuho) framed it directly: HD should "start to pull ahead" of LOW on comps in coming quarters partly because it laps its 2024 Pro deals while LOW is still absorbing 2025's.

Driver 2 — Trade-up, not turnover (proven, but has limits)

Both companies are growing average ticket (HD comp ticket +2.8% in Q2 FY26) while comp transactions are flat to down — customers doing smaller projects and buying pricier items per visit, not a broader volume recovery. This is a real but bounded driver: it cannot substitute indefinitely for the large-ticket, big-transaction renovation spend that only returns with housing turnover.

Driver 3 — Digital/omnichannel (proven, compounding)

Both online channels are growing double digits (HD +11%, LOW +15.7% in the same quarter) on a base that is still a minority of sales — a genuine, self-funding growth lever independent of the housing cycle.

Driver 4 — A housing-turnover recovery (optionality — zero in the base case for both)

Neither company is guiding to a housing recovery, and neither report assumes one in the base case. If mortgage rates fall meaningfully from ~6.95-7.1%, both stocks re-rate together; this is upside, not thesis, for both names, and it is the single biggest swing factor in either bull case.

Strip the noise: the IEEPA tariff refund is propping up both companies' "growth" narratives this quarter — and the effect is large enough to flip the sign.

The February 2026 Supreme Court ruling in Learning Resources v. Trump struck down tariffs imposed under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, and both retailers have been receiving refunds of duties paid on 2025 imports. Home Depot received $730M in refunds in Q2 FY26 (received almost entirely by late June), recognizing $685M of that in cost of goods sold. After-tax, at HD's ~24.3% effective rate, that is roughly $0.52 of the quarter's $4.92 adjusted diluted EPS. Strip it out and Q2 adjusted EPS is $4.40 — down 6.0% year over year, not up 5.1% as reported. Lowe's disclosed the effect directly: its $4.40 Q2 adjusted diluted EPS included an explicit $0.11 IEEPA benefit. Clean, that's $4.29 — down 0.9% year over year, not up 1.6% as reported. Both companies' full-year FY2026 guidance still embeds this non-recurring benefit (HD said the refund was "the vast majority" of what it expects to receive, essentially a one-time item concentrated in FY2026), which means the FY2026 guided EPS growth rate for both names overstates underlying earnings-power growth, and FY2027 comparisons will not have this tailwind to lap against.

Both companies' FY2025 free cash flow (operating cash flow less capex) tells a slightly different story than net income alone. Neither is mispriced cheap today; the case for owning either has to rest on the optionality in the bull case, not a value gap in the base case.

Fit with the portfolio's growth objective

Neither name is currently held — both would be genuinely new exposure, and both diversify away from the AI-infrastructure cluster (NVDA, AVGO, TSM, ASML, GRID, GEV, SOXQ, VIS) that already dominates this book's growth bets; home-improvement retail has essentially no correlation to that theme. Yield is close to irrelevant to this mandate given the $80,000 guaranteed pension — HD's 3.05% and LOW's 2.55% yields are a modest total-return contributor, not a reason to own either, though LOW's 63-year raise streak is a genuine quality signal about capital discipline through cycles, separate from the yield itself. Sequencing matters here: per the standing redeployment framework, closing the VXUS/AVUV shortfall (combined gap ~$179K vs. target, funded 2/3-1/3) remains the first priority for new capital ahead of any new individual equity position — a HD/LOW allocation should come from the funding order already established (idle cash → CRM exit → TLXIX trim → FSSNX/VTWO overlap) after that gap is being addressed, not instead of it. Tax location is a non-issue for either — ordinary growth equities with modest qualified dividends, fine in the taxable account.

The three things to actually watch

  • Lowe's Q3 FY2026 print (~mid-November 2026). The first real read on whether FBM integration and cross-selling are tracking to plan, five months after close. A clean print with reaffirmed leverage guidance is the single biggest de-risking event for the LOW thesis; a miss or another downward guidance revision (LOW already trimmed FY2026 guidance once, at Q2) would be the opposite.
  • Comparable transaction counts, not just ticket, at both companies. Both are currently growing revenue on trade-up (higher ticket) while transactions are flat-to-negative. The first quarter either company reports positive comp transactions is the first real evidence of housing-driven demand returning, not just price/mix.
  • Leverage trajectory vs. target at both companies' next two prints. HD targets 2.0x by end-FY2026 (this fiscal year); LOW targets 2.75x by mid-2027. Slippage on either timeline pushes out buyback resumption and the per-share EPS growth our base cases assume from FY2027 onward.

Bottom line

Home Depot is the better business: more mature Pro-distribution integration, closer to its own leverage target, a 55/45 Pro/DIY mix that gives it more resilience to a soft DIY consumer, and a cleaner recent cash-conversion story than the headline FCF decline suggests once you account for the inventory build from three acquisitions closing inside eighteen months. But at ~20-21x earnings that are themselves flattered by a one-time tariff refund, HD is not offering a full 10%/yr expected return at the probability-weighted midpoint of our own model — it needs either a better price or a genuinely stronger FY2027 than its own trailing five-year trend to clear that bar.

Lowe's is the better price: a cheaper multiple, a modestly better probability-weighted IRR, a 63-year dividend-raise streak that says something real about capital discipline through cycles, and a stock already within about 1% of what our model says it's worth at a 10% hurdle rather than the ~4% premium HD carries. The cost is a less mature, higher-execution-risk integration and materially more balance-sheet work left to do. My call: start a staged position in Lowe's now — the price already does most of the work an investor needs done — and treat Home Depot as the higher-quality name to build into on weakness, with the high-$280s/low-$290s (where our model clears a clean 10%+/yr) as the level that would turn HD from "wait" to "add." Neither should be sized past the standard 6-8% soft cap, and neither should jump the queue ahead of the VXUS/AVUV redeployment already in progress.
